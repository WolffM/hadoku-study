# Mesopotamian wisdom

## Overview

Mesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia, Assyria, roughly 3000–500 BCE) produced the oldest written wisdom literature we have. In Sumerian it is written as proverb collections and "instructions" from father to son. The **Instructions of Šuruppak** are attested in versions from c. 2500 BCE (Abu Salabikh, Fara) and in an Old Babylonian version c. 1800 BCE. In them the sage Šuruppak advises his son Ziusudra, the Sumerian flood hero. Akkadian-language wisdom includes the **Counsels of Wisdom** (Lambert's title; older scholars called it the "Babylonian Book of Proverbs" or "Precepts"), probably composed in the Kassite period (c. 1500–1200 BCE) and known from Assyrian library copies. There are also bilingual proverb collections and the great reflective texts _Ludlul bēl nēmeqi_ ("Let me praise the lord of wisdom") and the _Babylonian Theodicy_. None of these religions is practised today, so the main sensitivity here is scholarly accuracy rather than respect for a living community.

The ethical ideal centres on justice and right order, _kittum u mīšarum_ ("truth/steadfastness and equity"). Kings were expected to guarantee it, and the sun-god **Šamaš** (Sumerian **Utu**), who sees everything by day, enforced it as divine judge. The prologue and epilogue of **Hammurabi's laws** (c. 1754 BCE) state the royal side of this ideal: the king was called "so that the strong should not harm the weak", to protect "the widows and orphans", and to "enlighten the land", which already ties justice to light. The father-to-son instructions are practical and prudential: guard your tongue, avoid quarrels, don't stand surety, be careful with slander. Now and then they rise to lines of startling generosity, the best known being the Counsels of Wisdom command to repay evil with good.

On death, Mesopotamian thought is sober. The dead go to a dim underworld, and the hero Gilgamesh learns that immortality is reserved for the gods. The Old Babylonian Gilgamesh fragment's advice from the ale-wife Siduri (here "Sabitum") to enjoy food, clean clothes, family and the present day has long been compared with Ecclesiastes 9:7–9.

## Candidate passages

### 1. Code of Hammurabi, Prologue: "that the strong should not harm the weak"

> then Anu and Bel called by name me, Hammurabi, the exalted prince, who feared God, to bring about the rule of righteousness in the land, to destroy the wicked and the evil-doers; so that the strong should not harm the weak; so that I should rule over the black-headed people like Shamash, and enlighten the land, to further the well-being of mankind.

**Translation:** L. W. King, 1910.
**Source URL:** https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp
**Why:** One of the earliest statements that law exists to protect the weak, with justice pictured as sunlight ("like Shamash… enlighten the land").

### 2. Code of Hammurabi, Epilogue: widows and orphans

> That the strong might not injure the weak, in order to protect the widows and orphans, I have in Babylon the city where Anu and Bel raise high their head, in E-Sagil, the Temple, whose foundations stand firm as heaven and earth, in order to bespeak justice in the land, to settle all disputes, and heal all injuries, set up these my precious words, written upon my memorial stone, before the image of me, as king of righteousness.

**Translation:** King 1910.
**Source URL:** https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp
**Why:** Widows and orphans as the test of justice, the same concern found in the Hebrew prophets.

### 3. Code of Hammurabi, Epilogue: justice made public

> let the oppressed, who has a case at law, come and stand before this my image as king of righteousness; let him read the inscription, and understand my precious words: the inscription will explain his case to him; he will find out what is just, and his heart will be glad

**Translation:** King 1910 (excerpt; the sentence continues).
**Source URL:** https://avalon.law.yale.edu/ancient/hamcode.asp
**Why:** Law written on a public monument so the oppressed can read it. Justice "done in the open".

### 4. Counsels of Wisdom: repay evil with good

> In thine adversary not shalt thou place thy whole confidence.
> Unto him that doeth thee evil shalt thou return good.
> Unto thine enemy justice shalt thou mete out.

**Translation:** S. Langdon, _Babylonian Wisdom_ (1923), "The Babylonian Book of Proverbs", p. 90 (the section Langdon's introduction calls "§ G… which commands to reward evil with good"). Corresponds approximately to Lambert BWL, Counsels of Wisdom ll. 41–43 (line numbers approximate; not verified against Lambert).
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/babylonianwisdom0000slan/babylonianwisdom0000slan_djvu.txt
**Why:** Probably the oldest written command to return good for evil, perhaps a thousand years before Proverbs 25:21 and Matthew 5:44. Cut the first line for display.

### 5. Counsels of Wisdom: kindness rewarded by Shamash

> Give food to eat and wine to drink.
> Seek justice, feed and honor (parents?).
> In such a one will (his) god have pleasure.
> It is pleasing unto Shamash who will reward him with good.
> Render aid and be merciful always.

**Translation:** Langdon 1923, p. 90. Langdon's "(parents?)" is his own uncertainty. A footnote numeral was removed after "Render".
**Source URL:** archive.org Langdon djvu text (above).
**Why:** Feeding others and showing mercy, and "be merciful always" in particular, are compact and quotable.

### 6. Counsels of Wisdom: speak what is good

> Not shalt thou deal in slander, but speak what is pure.
> Evil shalt thou not utter, say what is good.
> Of him that deals in slander, speaking evil,
> Shamash shall wait for his head with recompense.

**Translation:** Langdon 1923, § M, p. 91.
**Source URL:** archive.org Langdon djvu text.
**Why:** Ethics of speech, a theme that runs through every wisdom tradition.

### 7. Counsels of Wisdom: guard your lips in anger (Barton's version)

> Thou shalt not make large thy mouth, but guard thy lip;
> In the time of anger thou shalt not speak at once.
> If thou speakest quickly, thou wilt repent (?) afterward,
> And in silence wilt thou sadden thy mind.

**Translation:** G. A. Barton, _Archaeology and the Bible_ (2nd ed. 1917), ch. XXII, "Precepts from the Library of Ashurbanipal", taken from Macmillan's translation (_Beiträge zur Assyriologie_ V). The same passage is Langdon's § N.
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/43070/pg43070.txt
**Why:** "Slow to speak, slow to anger" a millennium before James 1:19, from a clean, PD, Gutenberg-proofread text.

### 8. Bilingual Proverbs §21: everlasting treasure

> Evil thou shalt not do and so an everlasting treasure thou wilt obtain.

**Translation:** Langdon 1923, Bilingual Book of Proverbs § 21 (OCR "treaswre" corrected). Elsewhere in the same book (p. 26) Langdon quotes it as "…thou shalt obtain."
**Source URL:** archive.org Langdon djvu text.
**Why:** A one-line saying with a direct parallel to "treasures in heaven" (Matthew 6:20).

### 9. Old Babylonian Gilgamesh: Siduri's (Sabitum's) counsel

> "Why, O Gish, does thou run about?
> The life that thou seekest, thou wilt not find.
> When the gods created mankind,
> Death they imposed on mankind;
> Life they kept in their power.
> Thou, O Gish, fill thy belly,
> Day and night do thou rejoice,
> Daily make a rejoicing!
> Day and night a renewal of jollification!
> Let thy clothes be clean,
> Wash thy head and pour water over thee!
> Care for the little one who takes hold of thy hand!
> Let the wife rejoice in thy bosom!"

**Translation:** M. Jastrow Jr. & A. T. Clay, 1920 (from their introduction, which quotes the Old Babylonian "Meissner" fragment). "Gish" is their reading of Gilgamesh's name; "does thou" is as printed.
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11000/pg11000.txt
**Why:** The classic ancient answer to death: accept mortality and love the life in front of you. The editors note the parallel with Ecclesiastes. Cut to the last five lines for display.

### 10. Instructions of Šuruppak 242–244: things should serve you

> Nothing at all is to be valued, but life should be sweet. You should not serve things; things should serve you.

**Translation:** ETCSL t.5.6.1 (copyrighted; see table). The ETCSL line continues "My son, ......." (broken).
**Source URL:** https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section5/tr561.htm
**Why:** A strikingly modern line on possessions, about 4,000 years old. **Not PD.**

### 11. Instructions of Šuruppak 47 and 202–203: night, and the heart

> You should not travel during the night: it can hide both good and evil.

> A loving heart maintains a family; a hateful heart destroys a family.

**Translation:** ETCSL t.5.6.1 (copyrighted).
**Source URL:** https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section5/tr561.htm
**Why:** Line 47 is a practical but evocative darkness image. Lines 202–203 are a short aphorism on love. **Not PD.**

### 12. Instructions of Šuruppak 134–142 (excerpt): insults, greed, prayer

> Who insults can hurt only the skin; greedy eyes (?), however, can kill. […] Prayer is cool water that cools the heart.

**Translation:** ETCSL t.5.6.1 (copyrighted). "[…]" covers omitted lines, including one unclear line. "(?)" is ETCSL's own mark of uncertainty.
**Source URL:** https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/section5/tr561.htm
**Why:** "Prayer is cool water that cools the heart" is a beautiful standalone line. **Not PD.**

## Beware

- **"An eye for an eye" as the summary of Hammurabi:** the talion clauses (§§196–200) apply by social class. The prologue and epilogue framing (above) is more representative of the code's stated ideals. Don't present §196 as "Mesopotamian wisdom".
- **"First golden rule" claims:** the Counsels of Wisdom line is about returning good for evil (love of enemies), not the reciprocity golden rule. Keep the claim precise.
- **Popular Gilgamesh quotations** usually come from copyrighted modern versions (N. K. Sandars 1960, Stephen Mitchell 2004, a free re-creation, or Andrew George 1999). Use Jastrow & Clay or Thompson for PD text.
- **Older readings differ:** Langdon's and Barton's 1910s–20s readings sometimes differ substantially from Lambert's 1960 edition, and Langdon's attribution of the Counsels to the flood-hero Utnapishtim is his speculation. Present the PD lines as "an early translation", not the definitive text.

## Bridges

1. **Returning good for evil (Hebrew Bible: Proverbs 25:21–22; Christianity: Matthew 5:44, Romans 12:20–21).** Counsels of Wisdom ("Unto him that doeth thee evil shalt thou return good") is the earliest attested version of an idea that runs through Proverbs, Jesus, and Paul's "overcome evil with good".
2. **Death and the present moment (Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 9:7–10; Epicureanism: Letter to Menoeceus).** Siduri's counsel is almost the template for "Go your way, eat your bread with joy… Let your garments be always white." Jastrow & Clay point to Ecclesiastes themselves. The tension with Hávamál 77 (fame outlasts death) is worth noting.
3. **Justice as light (Christianity: John 3:19–21; Zoroastrianism: Yasna 44.5).** Hammurabi rules "like Shamash" to "enlighten the land", and Shamash the sun sees every deed. This parallels the light that exposes deeds in John 3 and the Zoroastrian dawn and noon as "monitors" of duty. Šuruppak's night that "can hide both good and evil" is the reverse image.
4. **Protecting widows and orphans (Hebrew Bible: Exodus 22:22, Isaiah 1:17, Amos 5:24; Islam: Qur'an 93:9).** The epilogue's royal duty matches the prophetic demand to "defend the fatherless, plead for the widow".
5. **Ethics of speech (Christianity: James 1:19; Hebrew Bible: Proverbs 10:19; Norse: Hávamál 29).** "In the time of anger thou shalt not speak at once" lines up with "slow to speak, slow to anger", and with Hávamál's "babbling tongue".
6. **Possessions (Stoicism: Epictetus, Enchiridion 1; Sufism: al-Ghazali's world-as-banquet).** Šuruppak's "You should not serve things; things should serve you" matches the Stoic distinction between what is and isn't "up to us", and al-Ghazali's foolish guest who tries to carry off the dishes (see sufism.md).
