# Transcendentalism (Emerson & Thoreau)

## Overview

New England Transcendentalism (roughly 1836-1860) was a loose movement of writers, ministers and reformers centred on Concord and Boston, Massachusetts. It grew out of Unitarianism, but the Transcendentalists broke with Unitarian reliance on historical evidence and scripture. They drew on German idealism (Kant, filtered through Coleridge and Carlyle), Romanticism, Neoplatonism, Quakerism, and early English translations of Hindu and Persian texts such as the Bhagavad Gita and the Laws of Manu. Their central claim is that every person has direct, intuitive access to divine truth ("Reason," in Emerson's Coleridgean sense, as against mere "Understanding"). The ethical consequence is a demanding inwardness: trust the inner light over custom, institutions and the opinion of the crowd, because the "Over-Soul" that speaks in conscience is the same in all people.

Key terms: **Self-Reliance**, which is trust in the universal soul speaking in you rather than egoism; the **Over-Soul**, the one mind that all individual minds share; **Compensation**, a moral law of balance in which every act carries its own reward or penalty; **Nature** as a symbol and teacher of spirit; and in Thoreau, **deliberate living**, voluntary simplicity, and the conscience that outranks the law. Light is the movement's master image: Emerson's "gleam of light which flashes across his mind from within", the soul as "not a faculty, but a light", Thoreau's "There is more day to dawn." This makes the tradition a close neighbour of John 3:19-21. Emerson, though, puts the light inside the person rather than in a person who has come into the world, which is exactly where he parted company with Christian orthodoxy (see his 1838 Divinity School Address).

Historical setting: Emerson's _Nature_ (1836) is the movement's founding text. _Essays: First Series_ (1841) contains "Self-Reliance", "Compensation" and "The Over-Soul". Thoreau lived at Walden Pond from 1845 to 1847, published _Walden_ in 1854, and published "Resistance to Civil Government" (later retitled "Civil Disobedience") in 1849, after spending a night in jail for refusing the poll tax in protest at slavery and the Mexican War. That essay later influenced Tolstoy, Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. The movement had real blind spots. It was largely white, male and Protestant-elite, although Margaret Fuller, Elizabeth Peabody and the Alcotts were central figures. Its "Oriental" sources were read through limited translations. Its individualism is in some tension with the communal ethics of most other traditions in this collection, which is worth bearing in mind for the Bridges below.

## Candidate passages

1. **Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841), para. 1**

   > A man should learn to detect and watch that gleam of light which flashes across his mind from within, more than the lustre of the firmament of bards and sages.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944 (plain text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2944/pg2944.txt)
   Why: the movement's core idea in one sentence, and it is framed as light.

2. **Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841)**

   > When we discern justice, when we discern truth, we do nothing of ourselves, but allow a passage to its beams.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944
   Why: a close echo of John 3:21 ("that his deeds may be made manifest, that they are wrought in God"). Truth is something we let through rather than something we produce.

3. **Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841)**

   > What I must do is all that concerns me, not what the people think. This rule, equally arduous in actual and in intellectual life, may serve for the whole distinction between greatness and meanness. It is the harder because you will always find those who think they know what is your duty better than you know it. It is easy in the world to live after the world's opinion; it is easy in solitude to live after our own; but the great man is he who in the midst of the crowd keeps with perfect sweetness the independence of solitude.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944
   Why: the classic statement of moral independence. The last sentence works on its own.

4. **Emerson, "Self-Reliance" (1841), closing lines**

   > Nothing can bring you peace but yourself. Nothing can bring you peace but the triumph of principles.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944
   Why: short and quotable. The second sentence corrects any purely egoistic reading of the first.

5. **Emerson, "The Over-Soul" (1841)**

   > From within or from behind, a light shines through us upon things and makes us aware that we are nothing, but the light is all.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944
   Why: the strongest light passage in the corpus. It pairs well with John 1 and John 3, and with Quaker "inner light".

6. **Emerson, "The Over-Soul" (1841)**

   > We live in succession, in division, in parts, in particles. Meantime within man is the soul of the whole; the wise silence; the universal beauty, to which every part and particle is equally related; the eternal ONE.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2944
   Why: a clear statement of the unity behind persons, close to the Upanishadic idea of atman as brahman.

7. **Emerson, _Nature_ (1836), ch. I "Nature"**

   > To speak truly, few adult persons can see nature. Most persons do not see the sun. At least they have a very superficial seeing. The sun illuminates only the eye of the man, but shines into the eye and the heart of the child.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29433 (plain text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/29433/pg29433.txt)
   Why: light, seeing and childlike perception, which recalls "unless ye become as little children".

8. **Emerson, _Nature_ (1836), ch. I**

   > Standing on the bare ground,--my head bathed by the blithe air, and uplifted into infinite space,--all mean egotism vanishes. I become a transparent eye-ball; I am nothing; I see all; the currents of the Universal Being circulate through me; I am part or particle of God.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29433
   Why: famous. It shows the humility, the dissolving of ego, that sits underneath "self-reliance".

9. **Thoreau, _Walden_ (1854), ch. 2 "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For"**

   > I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205 (plain text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/205/pg205.txt)
   Why: an iconic statement of examined living, framed against death.

10. **Thoreau, _Walden_ (1854), ch. 18 "Conclusion", final lines**

    > The light which puts out our eyes is darkness to us. Only that day dawns to which we are awake. There is more day to dawn. The sun is but a morning star.

    Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205
    Why: the closing words of _Walden_ and the best light/darkness pairing with John 3:19. Light we refuse to see is darkness to us.

11. **Thoreau, _Walden_ (1854), ch. 18 "Conclusion"**

    > If a man does not keep pace with his companions, perhaps it is because he hears a different drummer. Let him step to the music which he hears, however measured or far away.

    Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205
    Why: well known and gentle. It turns conscience into tolerance of other people.

12. **Thoreau, "Civil Disobedience" (1849)**

    > Under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison.

    Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71 (plain text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/71/pg71.txt)
    Why: "doing the truth" as public action, at a cost. This line runs directly into Gandhi and King.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:19-21 and John 1:9** ("the true Light, which lighteth every man that cometh into the world"). Emerson's Over-Soul is a universalised, de-Christologised version of the Johannine light. Passages 2, 5 and 10 resonate. The tension is that John locates the light in a person (Christ) and a choice for or against him, while Emerson locates it in every soul by nature.
- **Quakerism: George Fox's "Inner Light"** / "that of God in every one". This is the nearest Christian relative. Emerson admired Fox, and the Quaker idea of waiting in silence is close to the Over-Soul's "wise silence".
- **Hinduism: the Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita.** Emerson read the Gita in Charles Wilkins's 1785 translation, and his poem "Brahma" (1857) paraphrases Katha Upanishad 2.19 and Gita 2.19. "Within man is the soul of the whole" parallels Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7, _tat tvam asi_.
- **Stoicism: Epictetus, _Enchiridion_ 1** (what is "up to us"). Emerson's "Nothing can bring you peace but yourself" restates the Stoic dichotomy of control, and he read Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius.
- **Tension with Ubuntu** ("a person is a person through other persons") **and with Confucian role ethics**: Emerson's "great man... keeps... the independence of solitude" sits uneasily with relational personhood. That makes it a good teaching contrast.
- **Lakota: Ohiyesa, _The Soul of the Indian_ (1911)** on the solitary communion with the Great Mystery in nature, and on silence. Eastman explicitly wrote for readers formed by this Romantic-Transcendentalist sensibility.

## Beware

- **"What lies behind us and what lies before us are tiny matters compared to what lies within us"** is widely attributed to Emerson, but there is no source in his works. It is usually traced to Henry Stanley Haskins, _Meditations in Wall Street_ (1940).
- **"To laugh often and much... to know even one life has breathed easier because you have lived. This is to have succeeded"** is not Emerson. It derives from Bessie Anderson Stanley's 1905 poem "Success".
- **"Do not go where the path may lead, go instead where there is no path and leave a trail"** has no known source in Emerson.
- **"Go confidently in the direction of your dreams! Live the life you've imagined"** is a paraphrase. Thoreau's actual sentence (Walden, Conclusion) is "if one advances confidently in the direction of his dreams, and endeavors to live the life which he has imagined, he will meet with a success unexpected in common hours." I verified that wording in PG #205. Quote the original if it is used.
- **"Rather than love, than money, than fame, give me truth"** is a real Thoreau sentence (Walden, Conclusion), verified in PG #205. It is a possible extra candidate, but it is often garbled online, so copy it from the source.
