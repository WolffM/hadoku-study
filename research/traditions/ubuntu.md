# Ubuntu / Southern African ethics

## Overview

**Ubuntu** (Nguni: isiZulu, isiXhosa, siSwati, isiNdebele) and its cognate **botho** (Sotho-Tswana: Sesotho, Setswana, Sepedi) mean "humanness": the quality of being a real, morally developed person. The ethic is summed up in the maxim **umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu** (isiZulu) / **motho ke motho ka batho (babang)** (Sesotho/Setswana), "a person is a person through other persons". As Thaddeus Metz (fetched below) and Mogobe Ramose stress, this is a normative claim, not just an empirical one. Personhood is something one can achieve more or less fully, and one becomes a person by honouring communal relationships. These relationships combine **identity** (sharing a way of life, thinking of oneself as part of a "we") with **solidarity** (mutual aid and care). The highest good is social harmony. Hospitality, sharing, consensus-seeking (the _lekgotla_ or _imbizo_), forgiveness and respect for elders and ancestors follow from it. People who act with cruelty or indifference are said to lack ubuntu, _"akumuntu"_ ("he is not a person").

Ubuntu is rooted in the oral cultures of the Bantu-speaking peoples of Southern Africa. It was transmitted through proverbs (Zulu _izaga_, Sesotho _maele_), praise poetry (_izibongo_, _lithoko_), initiation teaching, and the customary law and practice of chiefs' courts. The _word_ "ubuntu" as a named philosophy is, however, largely a 20th-century development. It appears in Jordan Kush Ngubane and the Samkange brothers' _Hunhuism or Ubuntuism_ (Zimbabwe, 1980). It became a national ethic in South Africa's transition through the 1993 Interim Constitution's epilogue ("there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, a need for reparation but not for retaliation, a need for ubuntu but not for victimisation"), the Constitutional Court (_S v Makwanyane_, 1995, abolishing the death penalty), and Desmond Tutu's theology of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (_No Future Without Forgiveness_, 1999). The scholarly framing (Mbiti, Ramose, Metz, Gyekye) partly reconstructs and systematises an ethos that was lived rather than written. Some critics, such as Leonhard Praeg and Christian Gade, warn that "ubuntu" can be romanticised or used for nation-building rhetoric.

**Whose framing?** The earliest written collections of Southern African proverbs were made by European missionaries. Eugène Casalis of the Paris Evangelical Missionary Society, among the Basotho from 1833, wrote _Les Bassoutos_ (1859; English 1861). Others include Colenso and Callaway for Zulu, and later Sol Plaatje's _Sechuana Proverbs_ (1916), a Black South African author's collection. Casalis praises the proverbs while calling the Basotho "barbarous" and their land "the dark places of the earth". Read him as a sympathetic but thoroughly colonial witness.

**Cultural protocol.** Ubuntu proverbs and the maxim are public, widely shared and actively promoted by South African public institutions. There are no sacred-restriction concerns. Ancestral (_amadlozi_ / _badimo_) rituals and initiation (_lebollo_, _ulwaluko_) content, however, are restricted and not appropriate for display. Be careful to attribute proverbs to a specific language or people rather than a generic "African".

## Candidate passages

1. **The maxim, as stated in Metz (2011), §2.2 (CC BY 4.0)**

   > When Nguni speakers state 'Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu', and when Sotho-Tswana speakers say 'Motho ke motho ka batho babang', they are not merely making an empirical claim that our survival or well-being are causally dependent on others, which is about all a plain reading in English would admit. They are rather in the first instance tersely capturing a normative account of what we ought to most value in life.

   Source: https://www.ahrlj.up.ac.za/metz-t
   Original: _Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu_ (isiZulu); _Motho ke motho ka batho babang_ (Sesotho/Setswana), "A person is a person through other persons".
   Why: the maxim itself is the obvious display line. This passage gives a citable, openly licensed source for both language forms.

2. **Augustine Shutte, _Ubuntu: An Ethic for the New South Africa_ (2001), p. 30, as quoted in Metz (2011)**

   > Our deepest moral obligation is to become more fully human. And this means entering more and more deeply into community with others. So although the goal is personal fulfilment, selfishness is excluded.

   Source: https://www.ahrlj.up.ac.za/metz-t
   Why: a clear three-sentence summary of the ethic. (© Shutte. Short quotation only.)

3. **Desmond Tutu, _No Future Without Forgiveness_ (1999), p. 35, as quoted in Metz (2011)**

   > Harmony, friendliness, community are great goods. Social harmony is for us the summum bonum – the greatest good. Anything that subverts or undermines this sought-after good is to be avoided like the plague.

   Source: https://www.ahrlj.up.ac.za/metz-t
   Why: Tutu is the best-known exponent of ubuntu. (© Tutu estate. Needs permission for display beyond fair quotation.)

4. **Sesotho proverb no. 51, Casalis, _The Basutos_ (1861), p. 311**

   > "One link only sounds because of another." The natives wear little chains, which act as bells. This proverb implies, that we cannot do without the help of our fellow-creatures.

   Source: https://archive.org/details/basutosortwentyt00casa (text: https://archive.org/download/basutosortwentyt00casa/basutosortwentyt00casa_djvu.txt)
   Why: **a 19th-century, PD, Sotho image of ubuntu.** "One link only sounds because of another" is a lovely stand-alone line.

5. **Sesotho proverb no. 26, Casalis (1861), p. 309**

   > "If a man has been killed secretly, the straws of the field will tell it."

   Source: https://archive.org/details/basutosortwentyt00casa
   Why: hidden deeds come to light. A strong John 3:20 resonance. Casalis pairs it with no. 19, "The thief catches himself", glossed "The power of conscience is so great that it forces the thief to accuse himself".

6. **Sesotho proverb no. 38, Casalis (1861), p. 310**

   > "A good prince lights the fire for his people."

   Source: https://archive.org/details/basutosortwentyt00casa
   Why: servant leadership through an image of light and warmth. Short.

7. **Sesotho proverb no. 33, Casalis (1861), p. 310**

   > "Death does not know kings."

   Source: https://archive.org/details/basutosortwentyt00casa
   Why: death as leveller. Casalis compares Horace's _pallida mors_.

8. **Sesotho proverb no. 4, Casalis (1861), p. 307**

   Source: https://archive.org/details/basutosortwentyt00casa

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:19-21.** Casalis no. 26 ("If a man has been killed secretly, the straws of the field will tell it") and no. 19 ("The thief catches himself") speak of hidden deeds brought to light. Tutu's TRC theology linked ubuntu with truth-telling as the condition of reconciliation, "truth" being the first word of the commission's name. In John's terms, coming into the light.
- **Christianity: 1 Corinthians 12:12-26** ("the body is not one member, but many") and Romans 12:5. "One link only sounds because of another" pairs with Paul's body metaphor. Tutu explicitly read ubuntu through this lens.
- **Confucianism: relational personhood (_ren_, _Analects_ 6.30: "wishing to establish himself, he establishes others").** Both traditions define the self through relationships. Metz has written comparative work on ubuntu and Confucian ethics.
- **Lakota _mitákuye oyás'iŋ_ / Haudenosaunee "one mind".** These are related communal ontologies. The tension is that ubuntu focuses chiefly on human community, while Lakota relationality explicitly includes animals, plants and land.
- **Tension with Emerson's "Self-Reliance"** ("the great man is he who in the midst of the crowd keeps with perfect sweetness the independence of solitude"). This is the sharpest contrast in the collection between relational and individualist ideals of the person. Metz (2011) himself takes up the charge that ubuntu undervalues individual freedom.
- **Ancient Egypt: Ma'at as "connective justice"** (Assmann). Acting-for-one-another and reciprocity ("The knife that is lent does not return alone") match the Egyptian idea that deeds return.

## Beware

- **"I am because we are"** is real, but it is John Mbiti's 1969 paraphrase of African (chiefly East African) communal thought, not a traditional proverb. It is often misattributed to Tutu or Mandela or labelled a "Zulu proverb". The full Mbiti sentence is "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am" (copyrighted).
- **The "ubuntu" children's race story** ("an anthropologist offered a basket of fruit to the child who reached the tree first; the children held hands and ran together, saying 'ubuntu: how can one of us be happy if the others are sad?'") is an unsourced viral anecdote, probably from about 2009-2012. There is no documented anthropologist or tribe.
- **The Babemba "circle of praise" story** (a wrongdoer surrounded by villagers recounting his good deeds) is widely shared as "South African" or "Babemba" tradition. It has no ethnographic source, and the Bemba are Zambian, not South African.
- **"If you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go far, go together"** is labelled "African proverb" with no documented source in any African language.
- **"It takes a village to raise a child"** has a contested attribution, with many languages claimed. It is not specifically Southern African.
- **Mandela "ubuntu" video quotes** (e.g. about a traveller who would stop at a village and be fed) circulate in several transcriptions of a filmed interview. I did not verify them, so check the source recording before quoting.
