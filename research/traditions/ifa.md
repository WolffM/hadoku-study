# Yoruba Ifá & Yoruba proverbial ethics

## Overview

**Ifá** is the divination system and sacred literary corpus of the Yoruba of southwestern Nigeria, Benin and Togo. Through the Atlantic diaspora it also shaped Lucumí/Santería in Cuba and Candomblé in Brazil. It is attributed to **Ọ̀rúnmìlà**, the orisha of wisdom and witness to destiny. The corpus is organised into **256 odù** (signs). Each holds many **ẹsẹ Ifá** (verses), which are narrative poems recounting what diviners of old prescribed for clients human, animal and divine. A trained **babaláwo** ("father of secrets") memorises thousands of verses over years of apprenticeship. UNESCO inscribed the Ifá divination system as a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005, transferred to its Representative List in 2008.

The ethical centre is **ìwà**, character. The ideal person is the **ọmọlúwàbí**, roughly "a child of one who has good character". They are marked by **ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́** (gentle, cool character), honesty, respect for elders, hard work (_iṣẹ́_), generosity and self-restraint. Each person chooses or receives an **orí** (inner head, personal destiny) before birth. Good character is what allows that destiny to be realised, and bad character spoils even a good orí. The dictum **ìwà l'ẹwà**, "character is beauty", sums up the fusion of ethics and aesthetics, and Ifá narratives personify Ìwà as a woman whom Ọ̀rúnmìlà marries, loses through impatience, and must seek again (see Unverified). Proverbs (**òwe**) are the everyday vehicle of this ethics. A Yoruba saying recorded in the 19th century calls the proverb "the horse of conversation".

**Transmission and framing.** Ifá and the proverbs are oral. Ifá verses are chanted in archaic, highly allusive Yoruba, and many are esoteric, kept by initiates and recited for specific clients. Written collections began with missionaries and colonial writers:

- Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba-born Anglican bishop and formerly enslaved, whose _Vocabulary of the Yoruba Language_ (1843, enlarged 1852) contains proverbs;
- the American Baptist T. J. Bowen (1858);
- the British army officer A. B. Ellis (1894), who compiled English-only proverbs largely from those sources and framed them through colonial ethnology.

Serious Ifá text editions came only with Yoruba scholars of the 20th century. **Wándé Abímbọ́lá**, Awise Awo Agbaye (official spokesperson for Ifá), produced _Ìjìnlẹ̀ Ohùn Ẹnu Ifá_ (1968-69) and _Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus_ (1976). The American anthropologist **William Bascom** produced _Ifa Divination_ (1969). These are all in copyright, so **there is no satisfactory public-domain English edition of Ifá verses**. The verifiable PD material is proverbs.

**Cultural protocol.** Ifá is a living religion with initiatory knowledge. Many babaláwo regard odù verses as sacred speech meant for divination contexts, and some odù contain material considered restricted. Do not display verses detached from source and context, and prefer proverbs and the widely taught ìwà concepts. Diaspora practitioner websites and books (e.g. Fa'lokun Fatunmbi's _Iwa-pele_) are numerous but vary greatly in reliability. Use the academic editions above when checking an Ifá text.

## Candidate passages

All numbered proverbs are from A. B. Ellis, _The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa_ (London: Chapman & Hall, 1894), ch. XIII "Proverbs", pp. 218-229. Source: https://archive.org/details/b21781370 (text: https://archive.org/download/b21781370/b21781370_djvu.txt). I fixed OCR errors such as "tliem" → "them" and "tlie" → "the".

1. **Ellis 1894, proverb 3 (p. 218)**

   > He who has done something in secret, and sees people talking together, thinks they are talking of his action.

   Why: **the closest Yoruba resonance with John 3:20.** The guilty conscience fears exposure. Use it alongside proverb 2: "What is not wished to be known is done in secret."

2. **Ellis 1894, proverb 86 (p. 223)**

   > Wherever a man goes to dwell, his character goes with him.

   Why: ìwà as inseparable from the self. Short and universal.

3. **Ellis 1894, proverb 24 (p. 219)**

   > He who forgives ends the quarrel.

   Why: very short, and a strong candidate. It pairs with the Sermon on the Mount.

4. **Ellis 1894, proverb 71 (p. 223)**

   > Not to aid one in distress is to kill him in your heart.

   Why: a sharp moral psychology, close to 1 John 3:15-17 and Matthew 5:21-22.

5. **Ellis 1894, proverb 60 (p. 222)**

   > Time may be very long, but a lie will not go to forgetfulness.

   Why: truth outlasts concealment. It pairs with the Egyptian "Truth which is concealed shall be found".

6. **Ellis 1894, proverb 83 (p. 223)**

   > If a matter be dark, dive to the bottom.

   Why: a light/darkness image applied to inquiry. Seek out the truth of a dark matter.

7. **Ellis 1894, proverb 81 (p. 223)**

   > Ashes fly back in the face of him who throws them.

   Also in Bishop Vidal's introduction to Crowther's _Vocabulary_ (1852, introductory remarks, p. 19) as "Ashes always fly back in the face of him that throws them", glossed "the calumnies, or injuries, which one man aims at another will recoil on his own head."
   Why: a vivid image of moral boomerang, related to karma and Galatians 6:7.

8. **Ellis 1894, proverb 101 (p. 225)**

   > As a calabash receives the sediment of the water, so must an elder exercise forbearance.

   Yoruba (Crowther/Vidal 1852, unaccented orthography): _Agbe ni ije egbin omi_, "A calabash receives the sediment of water", explained as "An elder must exercise forbearance."
   Source for the Yoruba (introductory remarks, p. 19): https://archive.org/details/vocabularyofyoru00crow (text: https://archive.org/download/vocabularyofyoru00crow/vocabularyofyoru00crow_djvu.txt)
   Why: the one proverb here with a verified 19th-century Yoruba original. Leadership as the capacity to absorb.

9. **Ellis 1894, proverbs 22-23 (p. 219)**

   > Peace is the father of friendship.
   >
   > Strife never begets a gentle child.

   Why: the "gentle" (pẹ̀lẹ́) ideal, framed as kinship of virtues.

10. **Ellis 1894, proverb 134 (p. 228)**

    > Birth does not differ from birth; as the free man was born so was the slave.

    Why: a proverb of equal human dignity, preserved from a slave-holding society. It is powerful in context: Ellis's nos. 129-135 concern enslaved people.

11. **Ellis 1894, proverb 110 (p. 226)**

    > The dawn does not come twice to wake a man.

    Why: a dawn/light image about seizing the moment of awakening. It pairs with Thoreau's "Only that day dawns to which we are awake."

12. **Oyeleye, "Ìwà l'ẹwà: Towards a Yorùbá Feminist Ethics", _Yoruba Studies Review_ (2021), abstract**

    > ...the essay interrogates the ethical and aesthetical trajectory that leads from ìwà l'ẹwà (character is beauty), a Yoruba moral dictum, to ìwà l'ẹwà obìnrin ([good moral] character is a woman's beauty).

    Source: https://doaj.org/article/dc400407a07045bab8885ad695f82eb2 (DOAJ record of the open-access article)
    Why: scholarly attestation of the dictum **Ìwà l'ẹwà, "Character is beauty"**, which is the best short display text for this tradition. The article also gives a critical feminist reading worth knowing.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:20** ("every one that doeth evil hateth the light, neither cometh to the light, lest his deeds should be reproved"). Proverb 1 ("He who has done something in secret... thinks they are talking of his action") is a near-perfect psychological gloss. Compare also Proverbs 28:1, "The wicked flee when no man pursueth."
- **Ancient Egypt: Ma'at / _The Eloquent Peasant_** ("Truth which is concealed shall be found, and falsehood shall perish") makes a pair with proverb 5 ("a lie will not go to forgetfulness"). Both are West and North African traditions in which character is ultimately judged.
- **Greek philosophy: Heraclitus fr. B119, _ēthos anthrōpōi daimōn_** ("character is a man's destiny"). This sets up the Ifá relation of orí (destiny) and ìwà (character), where character determines whether destiny is fulfilled.
- **Confucianism: _Analects_ 4.1 and 12.1** (benevolence, _ren_). The ọmọlúwàbí resembles the Confucian _junzi_. Both are ideals of the cultivated, gentle, respectful person, defined through social relations and respect for elders (proverb 8).
- **Ubuntu.** Proverb 4 ("Not to aid one in distress is to kill him in your heart") and the communal emphasis of ọmọlúwàbí parallel _umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu_. Both are often grouped as "African communitarian ethics", though they come from different language families and regions.
- **Hinduism/Buddhism: karma.** "Ashes fly back in the face of him who throws them" (proverb 7) as a folk statement of moral return. Compare also Dhammapada 1-2.

## Beware

- **Generic "African proverbs"** such as "If you want to go fast, go alone; if you want to go far, go together" or "It takes a village to raise a child" are often labelled "Yoruba". Neither has a documented Yoruba source. The first appears to be a modern coinage of the 2000s. The second's attribution to a specific African language (Igbo, Yoruba and Swahili are all claimed) is undocumented.
- **"When there is no enemy within, the enemies outside cannot hurt you"** is labelled "African proverb". It has no documented origin, and its popularity owes much to Winston Churchill misattributions and motivational posters.
- **Odù verses on Instagram, Pinterest and diaspora sites** are often paraphrased, merged or invented, and some "Ifá says..." quotes are simply modern self-help. Verify against Abímbọ́lá or Bascom.
- **Ellis's proverbs** are genuine 19th-century records but English-only and sometimes "explained" through English equivalents. Ellis's parenthetical comparisons, such as "Many a mickle makes a muckle", are his, not Yoruba. Owomoyela 2005 is the best way to find tone-marked originals.

## Unverified (memory only)

- **The Ifá narrative of Ìwà, wife of Ọ̀rúnmìlà** (in Odù Ogbè Alárá per Abímbọ́lá 1975; Ìwà is daughter of Sùúrù, "Patience"). Ọ̀rúnmìlà mistreats Ìwà, she leaves, and he searches for her, which teaches that character must be cherished. I saw it only in search-result summaries citing Abímbọ́lá (1975, pp. 395-416). The verse text is in copyright and was not fetched.
- **"Ìwà l'ẹ̀sìn"** ("Character is religion") is a widely cited Yoruba saying, but I did not verify it against a reputable source.
