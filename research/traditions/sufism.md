# Sufism

## Overview

Sufism (_taṣawwuf_) is the inward, devotional and mystical dimension of Islam. It grew from the ascetic piety (_zuhd_) of 8th-century Basra and Baghdad into a vast tradition of teachers, orders (_ṭarīqa_, pl. _ṭuruq_), poetry and theology across the Muslim world. It is a living tradition with millions of practitioners today. It is also contested within Islam: some modern reformist movements reject many Sufi practices. Sufis themselves see their path as the fullest realisation of _iḥsān_, defined in the "Hadith of Gabriel" as worshipping God "as though you see Him". Presenting Sufism as a "universal spirituality" detached from Islam, as many Western popularisations do, misrepresents it. Rumi, Rabiʿa, al-Ghazali and Attar were all devout Muslims steeped in the Qur'an and hadith.

The ethical core is the purification of the self. The ego-self (_nafs_) with its greed, anger and self-regard must be disciplined (_mujāhada_, the "greater struggle") so that the heart (_qalb_) can become a polished mirror reflecting God. This image appears in al-Ghazali below. The goal is love (_maḥabba_, _ʿishq_) and direct knowledge of God (_maʿrifa_), culminating in _fanāʾ_ (passing away of the self) and _baqāʾ_ (subsisting in God). Several ethical consequences follow. Sufis value humility and awareness of one's own faults. They prize sincerity (_ikhlāṣ_), meaning worship and good deeds done for God's sake rather than for reward or reputation, which is the point of Rabiʿa's famous prayer. They prefer the "state of the heart" to outward forms (Rumi's Moses and the Shepherd). And they see death as homecoming, the reed returning to its reed-bed.

The four figures span five centuries. **Rābiʿa al-ʿAdawiyya** of Basra (d. 801, though some sources give 752) is known almost entirely through later hagiography, especially Attar's. **Abū Ḥāmid al-Ghazālī** (1058–1111) was the great jurist-theologian who left his Baghdad professorship for the Sufi path and wrote the _Iḥyāʾ ʿulūm al-dīn_ and its Persian abridgement _Kīmiyā-yi saʿādat_ ("Alchemy of Happiness"). **Farīd al-Dīn ʿAṭṭār** (d. c. 1221) wrote the _Manṭiq al-ṭayr_ ("Conference of the Birds") and the _Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ_ (Memorial of the Saints). **Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī** (1207–1273) of Konya wrote the six-book _Masnavī-yi maʿnavī_ and the lyric _Dīvān-i Shams-i Tabrīzī_.

## Candidate passages

Normalisation for archive.org OCR texts: collapsed OCR double spaces, removed footnote numerals, fixed obvious OCR slips (each noted). Gutenberg and Wikisource texts are copied unchanged.

### 1. Rumi, _Masnavi_ I, Prologue (the Song of the Reed), opening

> Hearken to the reed-flute, how it discourses
> When complaining of the pains of separation—
> "Ever since they tore me from my osier bed,
> My plaintive notes have moved men and women to tears.
> I burst my breast, striving to give vent to sighs,
> And to express the pangs of my yearning for my home.
> He who abides far away from his home
> Is ever longing for the day he shall return.

**Translation:** E. H. Whinfield, _Masnavi i Ma'navi_ (2nd ed., 1898), Book I, Prologue (Nicholson numbering I.1–4 approx.).
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0_djvu.txt
**Why:** The most famous opening in Persian literature: the soul as an exiled reed longing for home.

### 2. Rumi, _Masnavi_ II, "Moses and the Shepherd": God looks at the heart

> I created not men to gain a profit from them,
> But to shower my beneficence upon them.
> […]
> I regard not the outside and the words,
> I regard the inside and the state of heart.
> I look at the heart if it be humble,
> Though the words may be the reverse of humble.

**Translation:** Whinfield 1898, Book II, Story VII (God speaking to Moses after Moses rebukes a shepherd's naive prayer). "[…]" omits four lines.
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0_djvu.txt
**Why:** Sincerity over form, and humility of heart over correct words. One of Rumi's most loved stories.

### 3. Rumi, _Masnavi_ III, "The Elephant in a Dark Room"

> The eye of outward sense is as the palm of a hand,
> The whole of the object is not grasped in the palm.
> The sea itself is one thing, the foam another;
> Neglect the foam, and regard the sea with your eyes.

**Translation:** Whinfield 1898, Book III, Story V (p. 122). The story itself, as Whinfield summarises it: people feel an elephant in a dark room and each describes only the part touched. These lines are the moral.
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0_djvu.txt
**Why:** Partial knowledge in darkness, and the humility it demands. The best-known cross-cultural parable (see Bridges).

### 4. Rumi, _Masnavi_ I, "Joseph and the Mirror": confessing one's defects

> Whoso recognises and confesses his own defects
> Is hastening in the way that leads to perfection!
> But he advances not towards the Almighty
> Who fancies himself to be perfect.
> No sickness worse than fancying thyself perfect
> Can infect thy soul, O arrogant misguided one!

**Translation:** Whinfield 1898, Book I, Story XII (OCR "0 arrogant" → "O arrogant").
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0/masnaviimanavisp00jala_0_djvu.txt
**Why:** A pure humility passage, self-contained and quotable.

### 5. Rumi, _Masnavi_ III: "I died as mineral"

> “I died as mineral and became a plant,
> I died as plant and rose to animal,
> I died as animal and I was man.
> Why should I fear? When was I less by dying?
> Yet once more I shall die as man, to soar
> With angels blest; but even from angelhood
> I must pass on: all except God doth perish.
> When I have sacrificed my angel soul,
> I shall become what no mind e’er conceived.
> Oh, let me not exist! for Non-existence
> Proclaims in organ tones, ‘To Him we shall return.’”

**Translation:** R. A. Nicholson, _The Mystics of Islam_ (1914), ch. VI. Whinfield 1898 has another PD version in Book III, Story XVII ("The Vakil of Bokhara"), beginning "I died as a mineral and arose a plant". Commonly cited as _Masnavi_ III.3901–3906 (verse numbers from memory, not checked here). The last line quotes Qur'an 2:156.
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67388/pg67388.txt
**Why:** Death as ascent rather than loss. The first four lines stand well alone.

### 6. Rumi, _Divan-i Shams_, Ode XXIV: "To thee it seems a setting, but 'tis a rising"

> When my bier moveth on the day of death,
> Think not my heart is in this world.
> Do not weep for me and cry 'Woe, woe!'
> Thou wilt fall in the devil's snare: that is woe.
> When thou seest my hearse, cry not 'Parted, parted!'
> Union and meeting are mine in that hour.
> If thou commit me to the grave, say not 'Farewell, farewell!'
> For the grave is a curtain hiding the communion of Paradise.
> After beholding descent, consider resurrection;
> Why should setting be injurious to the sun and moon?
> To thee it seems a setting, but 'tis a rising;
> Tho' the vault seems a prison, 'tis the release of the soul.

**Translation:** R. A. Nicholson, _Selected Poems from the Dīvāni Shamsi Tabrīz_ (1898), Ode XXIV, p. 95 (OCR "rneetiiifTf" → "meeting"). F. Hadland Davis's _The Persian Mystics: Rumi_ (1907, Gutenberg #45159) reprints it slightly abridged.
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/in.ernet.dli.2015.52959/2015.52959.Selected-Poems-From-The-Divani-Shamsi-Tabriz_djvu.txt
**Why:** Rumi on his own death, built on a sunset/sunrise image. Strong for Death and Light. The last two lines alone make a fine display.

### 7. Rabiʿa: worship without fear or hope of reward

> “O God! if I worship Thee in fear of Hell, burn me in Hell; and if I worship Thee in hope of Paradise, exclude me from Paradise; but if I worship Thee for Thine own sake, withhold not Thine everlasting beauty!”

**Translation:** R. A. Nicholson, _The Mystics of Islam_ (1914), ch. IV. Claud Field (1910) gives another PD rendering: "My God, if it is from fear of hell that I serve Thee, condemn me to burn in hell; and if it is for the hope of Paradise, forbid me entrance there; but if it is for Thy sake only, deny me not the sight of Thy face."
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67388/pg67388.txt ; Field: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/24314/pg24314.txt
**Why:** The classic statement of disinterested love and sincerity (_ikhlāṣ_). Note that it reaches us through later hagiography (Attar), not Rabiʿa's own writings.

### 8. Rabiʿa: "I jest with it"

> Someone asked her one day, "Whence comest thou?" "From the other world," was her reply. "And whither goest thou?" "Into the other world." "And what doest thou in this world." "I jest with it by eating its bread and doing the works of the other world in it."

**Translation:** Claud Field, _Mystics and Saints of Islam_ (1910), ch. III "Rabia, the Woman Sufi" (from Attar's _Tadhkirat al-awliyāʾ_ via French). Field writes "this world." with a period where a question mark is expected; copied as printed.
**Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/24314/pg24314.txt
**Why:** Light-hearted detachment, and a short exchange that works on a card.

### 9. al-Ghazali, _Alchemy of Happiness_ ch. I: the heart as a mirror

> The aim of moral discipline is to purify the heart from the rust of passion and resentment, till, like a clear mirror, it reflects the light of God.

**Translation:** Claud Field, _The Alchemy of Happiness_ (1909), ch. I "The Knowledge of Self".
**Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=The_Alchemy_of_Happiness_(Field)/Chapter_I&action=render (page: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Alchemy_of_Happiness_(Field)/Chapter_I)
**Why:** One sentence summing up Sufi ethics: purification so that light can be reflected.

### 10. al-Ghazali, _Alchemy of Happiness_ ch. I: self-knowledge

> Knowledge of self is the key to the knowledge of God, according to the saying: "He who knows himself knows God," and, as it is written in the Koran, "We will show them Our signs in the world and in themselves, that the truth may be manifest to them." Now nothing is nearer to thee than thyself, and if thou knowest not thyself how canst thou know anything else?

**Translation:** Field 1909, ch. I (footnote marker "[1]" removed). The quoted verse is Qur'an 41:53. The "saying" is a popular Sufi maxim whose attribution to the Prophet is considered weak by hadith scholars.
**Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=The_Alchemy_of_Happiness_(Field)/Chapter_I&action=render
**Why:** "Nothing is nearer to thee than thyself." A clear bridge to the Delphic "Know thyself".

### 11. al-Ghazali, _Alchemy of Happiness_ ch. III: the world as a banquet

> The world is like a table spread for successive relays of guests who come and go. There are gold and silver dishes, abundance of food and perfumes. The wise guest eats as much as is sufficient for him, smells the perfumes, thanks his host, and departs. The foolish guest, on the other hand, tries to carry off some of the gold and silver dishes, only to find them wrenched out of his hands and himself thrust forth, disappointed and disgraced.

**Translation:** Field 1909, ch. III "The Knowledge of This World".
**Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=The_Alchemy_of_Happiness_(Field)/Chapter_III&action=render
**Why:** Gratitude, moderation and non-attachment in one homely parable.

### 12. Attar, _Manṭiq al-ṭayr_: the moths and the flame

> The Moths had long been exiled from the Flame
> They worship: so to solemn Council came,
> And voted One of them by Lot be sent
> To find their Idol. One was chosen: went.
> And after a long Circuit in sheer Gloom,
> Seeing, he thought, the TAPER in a Room
> Flew back at once to say so. But the chief
> Of Mothistan slighted so slight Belief,
> And sent another Messenger, who flew
> Up to the House, in at the window, through
> The Flame itself; and back the Message brings,
> With yet no sign of Conflict on his wings.
> Then went a Third, and spurr'd with true Desire,
> Plunging at once into the sacred Fire,
> Folded his Wings within, till he became
> One Colour and one Substance with the Flame.
> He only knew the Flame who in it burn'd;
> And only He could tell who ne'er to tell return'd.

**Translation:** Edward FitzGerald, _Bird Parliament_ (1889), ll. 1277–1294 (line numbers printed in the text; a page number was removed). Free paraphrase.
**Source URL:** https://archive.org/download/BirdParliamentEnglishFareeduddinAttaarFitzgerald/bird-parliament-english%20Fareeduddin%20Attar%20Fitzgerald_djvu.txt (the scan is of John Bruno Hare's sacred-texts.com edition, April 2007)
**Why:** Knowing the light only by entering it. The final couplet stands alone and bridges to John 3:21 ("comes to the light").

## Unverified / use with caution

- **Rumi, Divan Ode XXXI ("I am neither Christian, nor Jew, nor Gabr, nor Moslem… My place is the Placeless, my trace is the Traceless")**, verified in Nicholson 1898 p. 125 (same archive.org URL as entry 6). However, modern scholarship (e.g. Franklin Lewis, _Rumi: Past and Present_) reportedly notes that this ode is absent from Furūzānfar's critical edition of the Divan and may not be by Rumi. I didn't verify that claim here. It is also often misread as "Rumi rejected religion". Use only with a caveat.
- **Attar, _Bird Parliament_ ending ("Come you lost Atoms to your Centre draw, / And be the Eternal Mirror that you saw: / Rays that have wander'd into Darkness wide / Return, and back into your Sun subside.")**, verified in FitzGerald 1889, ll. 1426–1429 (same URL as entry 12). Left out of the main list only because 12 entries is the cap. Strong light/darkness candidate.

## Beware

- **"What you seek is seeking you."** Its provenance is disputed: widely shared as Rumi, sometimes linked to a passage in _Fīhi mā fīhi_, but I found no verified PD source with this wording. Don't use.
- **"The wound is the place where the Light enters you."** A modern paraphrase, flagged on quote sites as misattributed. Not found in Whinfield or Nicholson. Don't use.
- **"Out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing, there is a field. I'll meet you there."** This is Coleman Barks's free rendering (copyrighted) of a real ghazal. The Persian concerns _īmān_ and _kufr_ (faith and unbelief) in a specific Islamic mystical context, and Barks's version detaches it from that context.
- **"Come, come, whoever you are… ours is not a caravan of despair"** is popularly ascribed to Rumi. Scholars attribute this quatrain to earlier poets (often Abū Saʿīd-i Abū'l-Khayr). From memory; not verified here.
- **Barks generally:** much "Rumi" online is Barks paraphrase, which removes Qur'anic and Islamic references. Prefer Whinfield, Nicholson or Arberry for accuracy.
- **Rabiʿa's "torch and bucket of water"** (to burn Paradise and quench Hell). The anecdote is late and hagiographic, not from Rabiʿa herself, and Field's 1910 claim that she "was buried near Jerusalem" conflates her with another woman saint (the Jerusalem tomb tradition is disputed; she most likely died in Basra).

## Bridges

1. **Doing good without reward (Rabbinic: Pirkei Avot 1:3, Antigonus of Sokho, "be not like servants who serve the master for the sake of receiving a reward"; Enlightenment: Kant's _Groundwork_ on acting from duty rather than inclination).** Rabiʿa's prayer is the devotional form of the same insight.
2. **The elephant in the dark (Buddhism: _Udāna_ 6.4, the blind men and the elephant; Jain _anekāntavāda_).** Rumi's version (from Sanāʾī before him) makes the darkness the reason for partial knowledge. That also connects with John 3:19–21: what is in the dark is misjudged.
3. **The heart as a mirror / light (Christianity: John 3:21 and 2 Corinthians 3:18; Zoroastrianism: Hādōxt Nask's self made "still lovelier" by good deeds).** al-Ghazali's polished mirror reflecting "the light of God" and Attar's moth that becomes "one Substance with the Flame" are the Sufi versions of coming into the light.
4. **Know thyself (Greek: the Delphic _gnōthi seauton_ in Plato's _Alcibiades I_ and _Phaedrus_ 229e–230a).** al-Ghazali's "nothing is nearer to thee than thyself" deliberately joins self-knowledge to knowledge of God.
5. **God looks on the heart (Hebrew Bible: 1 Samuel 16:7, "the LORD looks at the heart"; Islam: hadith in Muslim, "Allah does not look at your forms… but at your hearts").** Rumi's Moses and the Shepherd is a narrative version.
6. **Death: two answers (Epicureanism: "death is nothing to us", Letter to Menoeceus; Norse: Hávamál 77).** Rumi's "To thee it seems a setting, but 'tis a rising" and "When was I less by dying?" is a mystic's homecoming, in tension with Epicurean annihilation and Norse survival-through-fame. Al-Ghazali's banquet guest who "thanks his host, and departs" is very close to Lucretius' image (_De Rerum Natura_ III) of leaving life like a satisfied dinner guest.
