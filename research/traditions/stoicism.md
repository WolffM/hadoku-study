# Stoicism

## Overview

Stoicism was founded in Athens around 300 BCE by Zeno of Citium, who taught in the Painted Porch (Στοὰ Ποικίλη, _Stoa Poikilē_). Chrysippus developed it into a system. The early Stoic writings survive only in fragments. The texts people read today come from the **Roman Stoics** of the 1st–2nd centuries CE: **Seneca** (c. 4 BCE–65 CE), statesman and adviser to Nero, who was forced to commit suicide; **Musonius Rufus** (c. 30–100 CE), a teacher exiled several times; his student **Epictetus** (c. 50–135 CE), a former slave whose lectures were written down by Arrian; and the emperor **Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE). Marcus's private notebook, _Ta eis heauton_ ("To Himself"), is known as the _Meditations_.

The core ethical claim is that **virtue** (ἀρετή, _aretē_) is the only true good, and vice the only true evil. Health, wealth, reputation, and even life itself are "indifferents" (ἀδιάφορα, _adiaphora_). Some are "preferred", but none of them is good in itself. Epictetus sharpens this into the **dichotomy of control**: some things are "up to us" (ἐφ' ἡμῖν, _eph' hēmin_), namely our judgments, impulses, and desires, and everything else is not. Distress comes from our judgments (δόγματα, _dogmata_) about things, not from the things themselves. The goal is to live "in agreement with nature" (ὁμολογουμένως τῇ φύσει ζῆν). Since nature is rational and ordered by the divine **logos** (λόγος), that means living by reason. The outcome is freedom from disturbing passions (ἀπάθεια, _apatheia_) and a smoothly flowing life (εὔροια βίου).

Stoicism is not only an inner discipline. It is strongly **cosmopolitan** and social. All rational beings share in the logos and are kin, "citizens of the world" (κοσμοπολίτης). The Stoic doctrine of **oikeiōsis** (οἰκείωσις, "appropriation" or "making-one's-own") describes concern widening from oneself to family, city, and humanity. That is why Seneca can say "we are the parts of one great body" and Marcus can say "men exist for the sake of one another." The early Christians found much that was congenial in the Stoics. A spurious correspondence between Seneca and Paul circulated in late antiquity, and the vocabulary of conscience, the indwelling divine, and universal kinship runs through both traditions.

## Candidate passages

Text was copied from the fetched files. Line breaks were joined, and Wikisource paragraph markers and zero-width spaces were removed. Curly apostrophes and quotation marks may have been typed as straight ones.

### 1. Epictetus, _Enchiridion_ 5

> Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. Thus death is nothing terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death, that it is terrible.

- **Translation:** Higginson (1865), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45109/pg45109.txt
- **Why:** This is the central Stoic insight. It also bridges to the Greek dossier through Socrates' calm before death.

### 2. Epictetus, _Enchiridion_ 43

> Everything has two handles: one by which it may be borne, another by which it cannot. If your brother acts unjustly, do not lay hold on the affair by the handle of his injustice, for by that it cannot be borne, but rather by the opposite—that he is your brother, that he was brought up with you; and thus you will lay hold on it as it is to be borne.

- **Translation:** Higginson (1865), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45109/pg45109.txt
- **Why:** It is concrete, gentle, and memorable: forbearance through reframing.

### 3. Epictetus, _Discourses_ I.14.13–14 (Crossley's _Golden Sayings_ §XXXVII)

> So when you have shut the doors and made a darkness within, remember never to say that you are alone; for you are not alone, but God is within, and your Guardian Spirit, and what light do they need to behold what you do?

- **Translation:** Hastings Crossley (1903), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/871/pg871.txt
- **Why:** This is the best bridge in the dossier to John 3:19–21. Darkness hides nothing from the divine witness within.

### 4. Seneca, _Moral Letters_ 43.4–5

> I shall mention a fact by which you may weigh the worth of a man's character: you will scarcely find anyone who can live with his door wide open. It is our conscience, not our pride, that has put doorkeepers at our doors; we live in such a fashion that being suddenly disclosed to view is equivalent to being caught in the act. What profits it, however, to hide ourselves away, and to avoid the eyes and ears of men? A good conscience welcomes the crowd, but a bad conscience, even in solitude, is disturbed and troubled. If your deeds are honourable, let everybody know them; if base, what matters it that no one knows them, as long as you yourself know them?

- **Translation:** Gummere (Loeb, 1917), PD
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_43
- **Why:** This is almost a pagan commentary on John 3:20–21: the doer of good comes to the light and the doer of evil hides. "A good conscience welcomes the crowd" can stand alone.

### 5. Seneca, _Moral Letters_ 10.5

> Do you not think, then, that some such wholesome advice as this could be given you: "Live among men as if God beheld you; speak with God as if men were listening"?

- **Translation:** Gummere (Loeb, 1917), PD
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_10
- **Why:** The integrity of public and private life fits in one symmetrical line. It belongs to the same "nothing to hide" theme.

### 6. Seneca, _Moral Letters_ 41.1–2

> God is near you, he is with you, he is within you. This is what I mean, Lucilius: a holy spirit indwells within us, one who marks our good and bad deeds, and is our guardian. As we treat this spirit, so are we treated by it.

- **Translation:** Gummere (Loeb, 1917), PD
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_41
- **Why:** It is strikingly close to Pauline language (1 Cor 3:16). Note that "holy spirit" here translates Latin _sacer spiritus_, not the Christian Holy Spirit.

### 7. Seneca, _Moral Letters_ 47.11 (on master and slave)

> But this is the kernel of my advice: Treat your inferiors as you would be treated by your betters.

- **Translation:** Gummere (Loeb, 1917), PD
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_47
- **Why:** This is a Stoic form of the golden rule, applied to the hardest case in Roman society, which was slavery. Caveat: Seneca urged humane treatment of slaves, not abolition, and he was himself a slaveholder.

### 8. Seneca, _Moral Letters_ 95.52

> all that you behold, that which comprises both god and man, is one—we are the parts of one great body. Nature produced us related to one another, since she created us from the same source and to the same end. She engendered in us mutual affection, and made us prone to friendships.

- **Translation:** Gummere (Loeb, 1925), PD
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_95
- **Why:** This is Stoic cosmopolitanism and kinship. It parallels Paul's "one body" (1 Cor 12, Rom 12:4–5).

### 9. Marcus Aurelius, _Meditations_ II.1

> Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busybody, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil. But I who have seen the nature of the good that it is beautiful, and of the bad that it is ugly, and the nature of him who does wrong, that it is akin to me [...] I can neither be injured by any of them, for no one can fix on me what is ugly, nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him. For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth.

- **Translation:** George Long (1862), PD. "[...]" marks my elision of a clause and Long's bracketed supplements.
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt
- **Why:** This is the most famous _Meditations_ entry and it is genuinely Marcus. It shows wrongdoing as ignorance, and the wrongdoer as kin.

### 10. Marcus Aurelius, _Meditations_ V.6

> A third in a manner does not even know what he has done, but he is like a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more after it has once produced its proper fruit. As a horse when he has run, a dog when he has tackled the game, a bee when it has made the honey, so a man when he has done a good act does not call out for others to come and see, but he goes on to another act, as a vine goes on to produce again the grapes in season.

- **Translation:** George Long (1862), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt
- **Why:** This is doing good without display. It is the Stoic counterpart to Matthew 6:3 ("don't let your left hand know what your right hand does") and a nice counterweight to the "come to the light" theme.

### 11. Marcus Aurelius, _Meditations_ X.16 (with IV.17)

> No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such.

- **Translation:** George Long (1862), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt
- **Why:** It is the shortest possible ethics, a sentence that cuts against mere theory. A companion is IV.17 from the same source: "Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good."

### 12. Musonius Rufus, _Discourse_ 10 ("Will the philosopher prosecute anyone for personal injury?")

> For to scheme how to bite back the biter and to return evil for evil is the act not of a human being but of a wild beast, which is incapable of reasoning that the majority of wrongs are done to men through ignorance and misunderstanding, from which man will cease as soon as he has been taught. But to accept injury not in a spirit of savage resentment and to show ourselves not implacable toward those who wrong us, but rather to be a source of good hope to them is characteristic of a benevolent and civilized way of life.

- **Translation:** Cora E. Lutz (1947). **Copyright: see the flag above.**
- **Source URL:** https://stoicbreviary.blogspot.com/p/musonius-rufus.html (a transcription of Lutz)
- **Why:** This is the Stoic teaching of non-retaliation, contemporary with the Gospels. "A source of good hope to them" is remarkable.

**Also verified (short alternates):**

- Marcus VI.6 (Long): "The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like [the wrong-doer]." The brackets are Long's.
- Marcus VIII.59 (Long): "Men exist for the sake of one another. Teach them then, or bear with them."
- Marcus XII.17 (Long): "If it is not right, do not do it: if it is not true, do not say it."
- Musonius, Fragment 51 (preserved by Aulus Gellius 16.1; Lutz): "If one accomplishes some good though with toil, the toil passes, but the good remains; if one does something dishonorable with pleasure, the pleasure passes, but the dishonor remains."
- Seneca, _Ep._ 1.3 (Gummere): "Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time."
- Epictetus, _Discourses_ III.22 (Long, on the Cynic), from https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10661/pg10661.txt: "For he ought not to wish to hide anything that he does".

## Beware (common misattributions)

Marcus Aurelius and Seneca are among the most heavily misquoted ancient authors online.

- **"Live a good life. If there are gods and they are just, then they will not care how devout you have been..."** This is **not in the _Meditations_**. It is a modern composition. The genuinely related passage is II.11, and its wording is quite different.
- **"Everything we hear is an opinion, not a fact. Everything we see is a perspective, not the truth."** This is **not in the _Meditations_**.
- **"Waste no more time arguing what a good man should be. Be one."** This is Gregory Hays's (2002, copyrighted) rendering of X.16. The PD Long wording is in #11 above.
- **"Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity."** This is attributed to Seneca, but no source has been found in his works.
- **"It's not what happens to you, but how you react to it that matters."** This is a modern paraphrase of _Ench._ 5, presented as a quote.
- **"We suffer more often in imagination than in reality."** This one is **genuine**. It is Gummere's rendering of Seneca _Ep._ 13.4 ("There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality."), verified at https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_13. It is listed here because people often assume it is fake. It is safe to use with the citation.

## Bridges

1. **Darkness that hides nothing. Epictetus _Disc._ I.14 and Seneca _Ep._ 43 ↔ Christianity, John 3:19–21 (already chosen); Islam, Qur'an 57:4 (God is with you wherever you are; see the islam dossier for verified wording).** Seneca's "a good conscience welcomes the crowd, but a bad conscience, even in solitude, is disturbed" sits very close to John's "everyone who does evil hates the light". In both, hiding is the symptom and the deed is the disease.
2. **Doing good without display. Marcus V.6 (the vine) ↔ Christianity, Matthew 6:1–4 (alms in secret); Daoism, _Daodejing_ 2 (the sage accomplishes and does not claim credit).** This is a tension with #1: the good act should be _able_ to bear the light, but it should not _seek_ an audience.
3. **The golden rule. Seneca _Ep._ 47.11 ↔ Rabbinic, Hillel in b. Shabbat 31a; Christianity, Matthew 7:12; Confucianism, _Analects_ 15.24; Islam, Nawawi's Forty #13.** Seneca's version is notable for applying the rule across a hierarchy (master and slave) rather than between equals.
4. **Non-retaliation. Musonius _Disc._ 10 and Marcus VI.6 ↔ Greek, Plato _Crito_ 49c; Christianity, Romans 12:17–21; Buddhism, _Dhammapada_ 3–5.**
5. **One body. Seneca _Ep._ 95.52, Marcus II.1 ↔ Christianity, 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Islam, the hadith of believers as one body (Bukhari/Muslim; see the islam dossier).** Note the difference: Seneca's body is all humanity and the gods, while Paul's is the church.
6. **Facing death. _Ench._ 5 and Marcus IV.17 ↔ Epicureanism, _Letter to Menoeceus_ 124–125.** The two rival schools reach the same calm by opposite routes: providence and the soul's virtue on one side, atoms and annihilation on the other.
