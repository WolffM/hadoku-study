# Zoroastrianism

## Overview

Zoroastrianism goes back to the prophet Zarathuštra (Greek Zoroaster), a priest-poet of eastern Iran. His dates are unknown. Scholarly estimates run from roughly 1500–1000 BCE (on the evidence of the archaic Old Avestan language) to the 7th–6th century BCE. Its oldest scripture is the **Gāθās**, seventeen hymns traditionally ascribed to Zarathuštra himself, embedded in the liturgy of the _Yasna_ (Y 28–34, 43–51, 53). Around them stand the later "Younger Avesta" (the rest of the Yasna, the _Yašts_, the _Vīdēvdād_, fragments such as the _Hādōxt Nask_). There is also a large body of Middle Persian (Pahlavi) theology and wisdom literature, mostly edited in the 9th–10th centuries CE (_Dēnkard_, _Bundahišn_, _Mēnōg ī Xrad_, _Šāyast nē Šāyast_). The tradition is alive today among the Parsis of India and the Zoroastrians of Iran and their diaspora. Please treat it as a living faith, not a museum piece. Zoroastrians object to the old label "fire-worshippers": fire (_ātar_) is the visible symbol of Truth and of Ahura Mazdā's light, not the object of worship.

The ethical core is a **cosmic choice between Truth and the Lie**. _Aša_ (Pahlavi _ahlāyīh_; truth, right order, righteousness) stands against _druj_ (the Lie, deceit, disorder). The Gathas describe two primal spirits: _Spəṇta Mainyu_, the bounteous or holy spirit, and the hostile spirit, later called _Aŋra Mainyu_ (Ahriman). Each "chose": one chose Truth, the other the worst. Every human being must likewise choose "each individually for himself" (Y 30.2). The choice is lived out in the triad _humata, hūxta, hvaršta_: good thoughts, good words, good deeds. In the Younger Avesta and in Pahlavi tradition, these are weighed after death at the _Činvatō pərətu_ (Chinvat Bridge, the "bridge of the separator"). There the soul (_urvan_) meets its own _daēnā_, its inner self or conscience, which appears as beautiful or hideous according to its deeds. History ends in _frašō.kərəti_, the "making wonderful" or renovation of the world, when the Lie is defeated.

The Gathas also name a group of divine qualities or "Bounteous Immortals" (_Aməša Spəṇtas_): _Vohu Manah_ (Good Mind), _Aša Vahišta_ (Best Truth), _Xšaθra_ (Dominion), _Ārmaiti_ (Devotion/Piety), _Haurvatāt_ (Wholeness) and _Amərətāt_ (Immortality). The Gathas are among the hardest texts in any Indo-European language. Every translation, especially the 19th-century ones, involves heavy interpretation. Mills's bracketed insertions below show that openly.

## Candidate passages

Gatha and Yasna passages from SBE 31 come from the Internet Archive OCR text of the 1887 edition:
`https://archive.org/download/wg931/WG931-1887%20-The%20Sacred%20Books%20of%20East%20-%20Vol%2031%20of%2050%20-Zoroastrianism-Zend%20Avesta%20-Part%203%20of%203_djvu.txt` (item page https://archive.org/details/wg931).
Normalisation: removed footnote numerals, rejoined words hyphenated across lines ("indi¬ vidually" → "individually"), joined text across page breaks, fixed one OCR slip (noted). Mills's parentheses are his own and are kept.

### 1. Yasna 30.2: each person must choose for themselves (Gatha)

> Hear ye then with your ears; see ye the bright flames with the (eyes of the) Better Mind. It is for a decision as to religions, man and man, each individually for himself. Before the great effort of the cause, awake ye (all) to our teaching!

**Translation:** L. H. Mills, SBE 31 (1887), p. 29.
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text (above).
**Why:** Personal moral choice, made in the light of "bright flames". Moral responsibility is individual.

### 2. Yasna 30.3: the two spirits, better and worse (Gatha)

> Thus are the primeval spirits who as a pair (combining their opposite strivings), and (yet each) independent in his action, have been famed (of old). (They are) a better thing, they two, and a worse, as to thought, as to word, and as to deed. And between these two let the wisely acting choose aright. (Choose ye) not (as) the evil-doers!

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, p. 29.
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** The founding text of ethical dualism, with the thought/word/deed triad already present. For display, consider only the last two sentences.

### 3. Yasna 30.5: the bounteous spirit chose Righteousness (Gatha)

> (Then when they had finished each his part in the deeds of creation, they chose distinctly each his separate realm.) He who was the evil of them both (chose the evil), thereby working the worst of possible results, but the more bounteous spirit chose the (Divine) Righteousness; (yea, He so chose) who clothes upon Himself the firm stones of heaven (as His robe). And He chose likewise them who content Ahura with actions, which (are performed) really in accordance with the faith.

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, pp. 30–31 (OCR "H e" → "He").
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** Even the divine is described as choosing. Long and bracket-heavy, so it's a reference passage more than a display one.

### 4. Yasna 44.3: "This I ask Thee, O Ahura! tell me aright" (Gatha)

> This I ask Thee, O Ahura! tell me aright: Who by generation was the first father of the Righteous Order (within the world)? Who gave the (recurring) sun and stars their (undeviating) way? Who established that whereby the moon waxes, and whereby she wanes, save Thee? These things, O Great Creator! would I know, and others likewise still.

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, p. 113.
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** The prophet's questioning, Job-like wonder. Rare in scripture: a prophet asking questions rather than issuing commands.

### 5. Yasna 44.5: who made the lights and the darkness? (Gatha)

> This I ask Thee, O Ahura! tell me aright; who, as a skilful artisan, hath made the lights and the darkness? Who, as thus skilful, hath made sleep and the zest (of waking hours)? Who (spread) the Auroras, the noontides and midnight, monitors to discerning (man), duty's true (guides)?

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, p. 114.
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** Light and darkness alike come from the Wise Lord. Dawn, noon and midnight are called "monitors" of duty. A key light/darkness passage and a direct bridge to Isaiah 45:7.

### 6. Yasna 45.2: the two spirits share nothing (Gatha)

> Yea, I will declare the world's two first spirits, of whom the more bountiful thus spake to the harmful: Neither our thoughts, nor commands, nor our understandings, nor our beliefs, nor our deeds, nor our consciences, nor our souls, are at one.

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, pp. 125–126 (joined across a page break).
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** A stark statement that good and evil cannot be reconciled, with a strong rhetorical rhythm.

### 7. Yasna 12.8: the creed of good thought, word and deed

> A Mazda-worshipper I am, of Zarathustra's order; (so) do I confess, as a praiser and confessor, and I therefore praise aloud the well-thought thought, the word well spoken, and the deed well done;

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, p. 250 (Mills's heading: "Yasna XII (Sr. XIII). The Mazdayasnian Confession"; OCR "Zarathmtra" → "Zarathustra"). The trailing semicolon is Mills's; v. 9 continues.
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** The confession (_Fravarāne_) still recited in the daily _kusti_ ritual. It is the canonical source of _humata, hūxta, hvaršta_. For display: "I praise aloud the well-thought thought, the word well spoken, and the deed well done."

### 8. Ashem Vohu: the prayer of Truth

> A blessing is Righteousness (called) the Best.
> It is weal; it is weal to this (man),
> When toward Righteousness Best there is right.

**Translation:** Mills, SBE 31, as recited at Yasna VIII.1 (the prayer itself is standardly cited at Y 27.14).
**Source URL:** archive.org SBE 31 djvu text.
**Why:** The most-recited Zoroastrian prayer, roughly "Truth is the best good; happiness to the one who is truthful for the sake of best Truth." Mills's version is awkward, so consider it reference-only unless a clearer PD rendering turns up.

### 9. Hādōxt Nask 2.9–11: the soul meets its own conscience

> And it seems to him as if his own conscience were advancing to him in that wind, in the shape of a maiden fair, bright, white-armed, strong, tall-formed, high-standing, thick-breasted, beautiful of body, noble, of a glorious seed, of the size of a maid in her fifteenth year, as fair as the fairest things in the world.
>
> And the soul of the faithful one addressed her, asking: 'What maid art thou, who art the fairest maid I have ever seen?'
>
> And she, being his own conscience, answers him: 'O thou youth of good thoughts, good words, and good deeds, of good religion, I am thy own conscience!

**Translation:** James Darmesteter, SBE 23 (1883; American ed. 1898), "Hadhokht Nask" fargard 2. Darmesteter renders _daēnā_ as "conscience".
**Source URL:** https://www.avesta.org/fragment/hsbe.htm
**Why:** After death, a person meets their own deeds in human form. A powerful image of the self made by its own conduct. For display, verse 11 alone is enough.

### 10. Hādōxt Nask 2.14–15: "thou madest me still lovelier"

> 'I was lovely and thou madest me still lovelier; I was fair and thou madest me still fairer; I was desirable and thou madest me still more desirable; I was sitting in a forward place and thou madest me sit in the foremost place, through this good thought, through this good speech, through this good deed of thine; […]'
>
> 'The first step that the soul of the faithful man made, placed him in the Good-Thought Paradise; 'The second step that the soul of the faithful man made, placed him in the Good-Word Paradise; 'The third step that the soul of the faithful man made, placed him in the Good-Deed Paradise; 'The fourth step that the soul of the faithful man made, placed him in the Endless Lights.'

**Translation:** Darmesteter, SBE 23. "[…]" marks my omission of the rest of v. 14.
**Source URL:** https://www.avesta.org/fragment/hsbe.htm
**Why:** The good life is pictured as a self made more beautiful by good conduct, ending in "the Endless Lights". Strong for Light and Death.

### 11. Mēnōg ī Xrad 2.102–111: "in the end thy trust is on thine own deeds"

> 'Thou shouldst not become presumptuous through much treasure and wealth; (103) for in the end it is necessary for thee to leave all.
>
> 'Thou shouldst not become presumptuous through predominance; (105) for in the end it is necessary for thee to become non-predominant. […]
>
> 'Thou shouldst not become presumptuous through great connections and race; (109) for in the end thy trust is on thine own deeds.
>
> 'Thou shouldst not become presumptuous through life; (111) for death comes upon thee at last,

**Translation:** E. W. West, SBE 24 (1885), _Dînâ-î Maînôg-î Khirad_ ("Spirit of Wisdom"), ch. 2. Parenthesised numbers are West's verse numbers. "[…]" marks omitted vv. 106–107. The quotation ends mid-sentence at v. 111; the text continues with the fate of the corpse and the soul's ascent to the Chinwad bridge.
**Source URL:** https://www.avesta.org/mp/mx.html
**Why:** Humility before death in a clear refrain. "In the end thy trust is on thine own deeds" is the core line to display.

### 12. Šāyast nē Šāyast 13.29: the Zoroastrian golden rule

> The eleven stanzas of At-fravakhshya (Y44) are made up from the six chieftainships and the five accomplishments (farhang) owing to religion; one is thus, not to do unto others all that which is not well for one's self;

**Translation:** E. W. West, SBE 5 (1880), _Shâyast lâ-Shâyast_ (supplementary text) ch. 13 §29. The "(Y44)" is an editorial insertion on the avesta.org page and appears to be wrong: _At fravaxšyā_ is Yasna **45** (11 stanzas), while the preceding §28 treats _Tat thwā pərəsā_, Y 44 (20 stanzas). The text goes on to list the other accomplishments.
**Source URL:** https://www.avesta.org/mp/shayest.html
**Why:** An explicit negative golden rule in Zoroastrian sources, useful for bridging. Display only the core clause: "not to do unto others all that which is not well for one's self."

## Beware

- **Nietzsche's _Thus Spoke Zarathustra_** has nothing to do with Zoroastrian teaching, yet its lines are routinely captioned "Zarathustra/Zoroaster".
- **"Doing good to others is not a duty, it is a joy, for it increases your own health and happiness"**, widely attributed to "Zoroaster" online. I found no source in any Avestan or Pahlavi text. Treat as spurious.
- **"Good thoughts, good words, good deeds"** is genuine, but it is a liturgical formula (see Y 12.8 and the triad in Y 30.3), not a single saying of Zarathuštra. Cite the text, not "Zoroaster said".
- **Translation dependence:** paraphrased Gatha quotes online often come from copyrighted modern versions (Insler, Taraporewala, Azargoshasb) and differ widely. Always cite the translator.

## Bridges

1. **Choice between two ways (Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 30:15–19; Christianity: Matthew 7:13–14).** Y 30.2–3 ("let the wisely acting choose aright") parallels Moses setting "life and death, the blessing and the curse" before Israel and urging "choose life". Both frame ethics as one decisive personal choice.
2. **Light and darkness (Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 45:7; Christianity: John 3:19–21).** Y 44.5 asks who made "the lights and the darkness". Isaiah 45:7, addressed to the Persian king Cyrus, has YHWH say he forms light _and_ creates darkness. Many scholars read this as a deliberate monotheist answer to Iranian dualism. The Hādōxt Nask's "Endless Lights" and "bright flames" of Y 30.2 resonate with the chosen John 3:19–21, where deeds done in truth "come to the light".
3. **Judgement by one's own deeds (Islam: Qur'an 99:7–8; Ancient Egypt: weighing of the heart; Norse: Hávamál 77).** The Hādōxt Nask's _daēnā_ (one's deeds met in human form) and the Mēnōg ī Xrad's "thy trust is on thine own deeds" match the Qur'an's "whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it". The Norse "verdict on each dead man" (_dómr um dauðan hvern_) is a secular counterpart.
4. **Golden rule (Rabbinic: Hillel, Shabbat 31a; Christianity: Matthew 7:12; Mesopotamian: Counsels of Wisdom).** Šāyast nē Šāyast 13.29, "not to do unto others all that which is not well for one's self", is a negative golden rule very close in form to Hillel's "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour."
5. **Thought, word and deed (Buddhism: body/speech/mind; Christianity: the confession "in thought, word and deed").** The Zoroastrian triad has close parallels elsewhere. The Christian liturgical formula ("I have sinned… in thought, word and deed") is a striking structural echo; whether it is historically connected is unclear.
6. **Humility before death (Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 5:15; Stoicism: Marcus Aurelius on the vanity of fame).** Mēnōg ī Xrad 2 ("in the end it is necessary for thee to leave all") parallels "As he came forth from his mother's womb, naked shall he go again."
