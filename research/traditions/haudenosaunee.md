# Haudenosaunee Great Law of Peace (Gayanashagowa)

## Overview

The Haudenosaunee ("People of the Longhouse"), called the Iroquois by the French and the Five Nations by the English, are the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations. The Tuscarora joined in the early 18th century, making Six Nations. According to their own tradition, the Confederacy was founded when the **Peacemaker** (Dekanawida; many Haudenosaunee avoid saying his name outside ceremony and simply call him the Peacemaker) and **Hayonhwatha** (Aionwatha, "Hiawatha") brought the Good Tidings of Peace and Power to nations exhausted by blood feud. They also transformed the tyrant Tadodaho (Adodarhoh) of the Onondaga. Dates proposed for the founding range from the 12th to the 16th century. The Confederacy's own website says "time immemorial". The Grand Council still meets at Onondaga.

The ethical vision is political and relational. Peace (_skén:nen_) is not just the absence of war. It is a constructed order of "Peace and Strength" grown from clear minds and good words. Its central images, all fetched below, are these:

- the **Tree of the Great Peace**, a white pine, under which the nations sit;
- the **Great White Roots** extending in the four directions, so that any nation with a clean mind may follow them to shelter;
- the **Eagle** that sees afar;
- the **bundle of five arrows** that cannot be broken;
- the **burial of weapons** beneath the uprooted tree;
- the **one dish with no knife in it**, meaning shared hunting grounds.

Leaders (_royaner_, "lords" or chiefs) are nominated and can be removed by the clan mothers. They are charged to have skin "seven spans" thick against anger and criticism, and to keep in view "the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground." This is the textual root of the widely cited **Seventh Generation** principle, which the Confederacy's own website calls a "core value". The literal phrase "seventh generation" does not appear in Parker's 1916 text (see Beware).

**Transmission and framing.** The Great Law is an oral constitution, kept by memory and recited over several days by trained speakers. It is mnemonically tied to **wampum belts and strings**, such as the Hiawatha Belt. The English written versions are 19th- and early 20th-century compilations by Haudenosaunee men working with or for non-Native institutions:

- the **Seth Newhouse** (Mohawk) manuscript, revised by him through about 1885-1910;
- the **1900 "Chiefs' version"** prepared by a committee of chiefs of the Six Nations Reserve in Ontario, including the Seneca chief John A. Gibson;
- edited together by **Arthur C. Parker** (Seneca descent, archaeologist at the New York State Museum) as _The Constitution of the Five Nations_ (1916).

Parker's numbered "laws" are therefore a codification in an Anglo-legal format, shaped by early-20th-century Six Nations politics and by Parker's own aims. Some Haudenosaunee traditionalists regard these written versions as partial or flawed, and the full recitation remains the authoritative form. The well-known claim that the Great Law influenced the US Constitution has some acknowledgement (a 1988 US Congressional resolution) but the extent of the influence is debated among historians.

**Cultural protocol.** The Great Law is still the living constitution of a sovereign Confederacy. Its full recitation, the Condolence ceremony and the Longhouse religion (the Code of Handsome Lake) are ceremonial and should not be displayed casually. The civic sections below (the Tree, the roots, the duties of leaders, the arrows, the burial of weapons) are widely taught publicly by the Haudenosaunee themselves, for example on the Confederacy's "Symbols" page, and are the appropriate choices. Use "Peacemaker" in display text rather than the personal name.

## Candidate passages

All Parker passages come from the archive.org OCR text of the 1916 NY State Museum Bulletin 184 (https://archive.org/details/constitutionoffi00parkuoft; text: https://archive.org/download/constitutionoffi00parkuoft/constitutionoffi00parkuoft_djvu.txt). I checked the OCR wording, normalising only line-break hyphenation and obvious OCR glyph errors ("1 name" → "I name", "oflfensive" → "offensive", "Ijound" → "bound").

1. **Great Law §1 (Parker 1916, p. 30)**

   > I am Dekanawidah and with the Five Nations' Confederate Lords I plant the Tree of the Great Peace. ... I name the tree the Tree of the Great Long Leaves. Under the shade of this Tree of the Great Peace we spread the soft white feathery down of the globe thistle as seats for you, Adodarhoh, and your cousin Lords.

   Why: the founding image of the Confederacy. For public display, consider starting at "I plant the Tree of the Great Peace" to avoid the personal name.

2. **Great Law §2 (Parker 1916, p. 30)**

   > Roots have spread out from the Tree of the Great Peace, one to the north, one to the east, one to the south and one to the west. The name of these roots is The Great White Roots and their nature is Peace and Strength.

   Why: short and self-contained. Peace offered outward in all directions.

3. **Great Law §2, continued**

   > If any man or any nation outside the Five Nations shall obey the laws of the Great Peace and make known their disposition to the Lords of the Confederacy, they may trace the Roots to the Tree and if their minds are clean and they are obedient and promise to obey the wishes of the Confederate Council, they shall be welcomed to take shelter beneath the Tree of the Long Leaves.

   Why: an open invitation conditioned on a "clean mind". It has some kinship with John 3:21's coming to the light.

4. **Great Law §2, continued**

   > We place at the top of the Tree of the Long Leaves an Eagle who is able to see afar. If he sees in the distance any evil approaching or any danger threatening he will at once warn the people of the Confederacy.

   Why: vigilance and foresight as civic virtues.

5. **Great Law §24 (Parker 1916, p. 37)**

   > The Lords of the Confederacy of the Five Nations shall be mentors of the people for all time. The thickness of their skin shall be seven spans — which is to say that they shall be proof against anger, offensive actions and criticism. Their hearts shall be full of peace and good will and their minds filled with a yearning for the welfare of the people of the Confederacy. With endless patience they shall carry out their duty and their firmness shall be tempered with a tenderness for their people.

   Why: perhaps the finest description of servant leadership in the corpus. "Firmness tempered with tenderness" stands alone well.

6. **Great Law §28, installation charge (Parker 1916, pp. 38-39)**

   > Look and listen for the welfare of the whole people and have always in view not only the present but also the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground — the unborn of the future Nation.

   Why: the textual source of the Seventh Generation principle, verbatim from the 1916 PD text. This is the strongest candidate.

7. **Great Law §28 (same charge, preceding sentences)**

   > In all of your deliberations in the Confederate Council, in your efforts at law making, in all your official acts, self interest shall be cast into oblivion. Cast not over your shoulder behind you the warnings of the nephews and nieces should they chide you for any error or wrong you may do, but return to the way of the Great Law which is just and right.

   Why: accountability. Leaders must accept correction and "return to the way". This resonates with "he that doeth truth cometh to the light".

8. **Great Law §57 (Parker 1916, p. 45)**

   > Five arrows shall be bound together very strong and each arrow shall represent one nation. As the five arrows are strongly bound this shall symbolize the complete union of the nations. Thus are the Five Nations united completely and enfolded together, united into one head, one body and one mind. Therefore they shall labor, legislate and council together for the interest of future generations.

   Why: unity, again explicitly tied to "future generations".

9. **Great Law §65 (Parker 1916, p. 49)**

   > I, Dekanawida, and the Union Lords, now uproot the tallest pine tree and into the cavity thereby made we cast all weapons of war. Into the depths of the earth, down into the deep underearth currents of water flowing to unknown regions we cast all the weapons of strife. We bury them from sight and we plant again the tree. Thus shall the Great Peace be established and hostilities shall no longer be known between the Five Nations but peace to the United People.

   Why: the origin of "bury the hatchet". It parallels Isaiah 2:4 (swords into ploughshares).

10. **"Tradition of the Origin of the Five Nations' League," Six Nations Chiefs' version (Parker 1916, p. 103)**

    > We shall only have one dish (or bowl) in which will be placed one beaver's tail and we shall all have coequal right to it, and there shall be no knife in it, for if there be a knife in it, there would be danger that it might cut some one and blood would thereby be shed.

    Why: the "Dish With One Spoon" ethic of the commons. Many Ontario land acknowledgements still invoke it.

11. **Origin narrative, Newhouse/Parker (Parker 1916, p. 24)**

    > It shall be the power to abolish war and robbery between brothers and bring peace and quietness.

    Why: a one-line definition of the Great Peace.

12. **Haudenosaunee Confederacy, "Values" page (current; © Haudenosaunee Confederacy, not PD)**

    > In their decision making Chiefs consider how present day decisions will impact their descendants. Nations are taught to respect the world in which they live as they are borrowing it from future generations.

    Source: https://www.haudenosauneeconfederacy.com/values/ (fetched 2026-11-10)
    Why: the Confederacy's own present-day statement of the Seventh Generation value. It is authoritative, but display needs attribution and ideally permission.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:20-21 and Matthew 18:15.** §28's charge to accept the chiding of "nephews and nieces" and "return to the way of the Great Law" parallels the Johannine idea that the one who does truth does not fear exposure. Compare also Proverbs 9:8-9 ("rebuke a wise man, and he will love thee").
- **Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 2:4 / Micah 4:3** ("beat their swords into plowshares"). §65's burial of weapons under the replanted tree makes a very close pairing.
- **Ubuntu / Southern African ethics.** "One head, one body and one mind" and the one dish both express a relational, consensus-seeking personhood. The traditional consensus process (the council "fire", deliberation until one mind) parallels the Southern African _lekgotla_ or _imbizo_.
- **Confucianism: _Analects_ 2.1** ("He who exercises government by means of his virtue may be compared to the north polar star"). §24's servant-mentor leadership echoes the Confucian idea of rule by virtue. Both make rulers' inner temper a public matter.
- **Ancient Egypt: Ptahhotep §5** ("If thou be a leader... endeavour always to be gracious"). This is another instruction to leaders on restraint of anger.
- **Ecological ethics / Lakota _mitákuye oyás'iŋ_.** The Seventh Generation principle pairs with the Lakota "all my relatives" and the Māori _kaitiakitanga_ (guardianship). All three extend moral community across species and time.

## Beware

- **"In every deliberation, we must consider the impact on the seventh generation..."** is often presented as a direct quotation from the Great Law. It is a modern paraphrase. Parker's 1916 text says "the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground" and "future generations", with no "seventh". The principle is genuine Haudenosaunee teaching (see the Confederacy's Values page and Oren Lyons' talks), but the quoted wording is not a source text.
- **Longfellow's _Song of Hiawatha_ (1855)** has nothing to do with the Haudenosaunee Hayonhwatha. Longfellow attached the name to Ojibwe Nanabozho stories from Schoolcraft.
- **The "Chief Seattle speech"** ("The earth does not belong to man...") is Duwamish/Suquamish, not Haudenosaunee, and its popular form was written by screenwriter Ted Perry in 1971. It is often wrongly mixed into Great Law material.
- **The "Iroquois Constitution was the model for the US Constitution"** claim is overstated in popular sources. Use cautious wording ("influenced", "admired by Franklin").
- **"Two Wolves" / "Cherokee legend of the two wolves"** circulates alongside Iroquois quotes. It is an evangelical sermon illustration from about the 1970s-1990s (Billy Graham, 1978, is a commonly cited early version), not a traditional teaching.
