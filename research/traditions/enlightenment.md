# Enlightenment ethics

## Overview

Between roughly 1650 and 1800, European moral philosophy tried to ground ethics in human reason and human nature rather than in revelation or church authority. The aim was usually to _explain_ morality, not to abolish it. Two broad lines emerged, and both still shape modern ethics. The **sentimentalist** line ran through the Scottish Enlightenment: Shaftesbury, Hutcheson, **David Hume** (1711–1776), and **Adam Smith** (1723–1790). It located morality in feeling. Humans approve of benevolence and disapprove of cruelty through a natural capacity Hume called "humanity" and Smith called **sympathy**, meaning fellow-feeling with another's situation. Smith's _Theory of Moral Sentiments_ (1759) adds the **impartial spectator**, an imagined fair observer we internalise as conscience: "the man within the breast." Hume's _Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals_ (1751) traces virtues to what is "useful or agreeable" to oneself or others. The Scots wrote before the Industrial Revolution. Smith's _Wealth of Nations_ (1776) presupposes the moral psychology of _TMS_ and does not replace it.

The **rationalist** reply came from **Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804) in Königsberg. His _Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals_ (_Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten_, 1785) argues that the only thing good without qualification is a **good will** (_guter Wille_). Moral worth lies in acting from **duty** (_Pflicht_), out of respect for the moral law, not from inclination or for results. The supreme principle is the **categorical imperative** (_kategorischer Imperativ_): act only on maxims you could will as universal law, and treat humanity always as an end (_Zweck an sich selbst_), never merely as a means. Its ideal is a "kingdom of ends" (_Reich der Zwecke_) of autonomous rational beings who give themselves the law. _Aufklärung_ ("enlightenment") was for Kant humanity's emergence from self-incurred immaturity: _sapere aude_, "dare to know."

**John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873) came a generation after the Enlightenment proper and inherited its reforming programme from Bentham. _Utilitarianism_ (1861 in _Fraser's Magazine_, as a book in 1863) defends the **Greatest Happiness Principle**: actions are right as they tend to promote happiness, counting everyone's happiness equally. Mill distinguished higher from lower pleasures and claimed the golden rule as the "complete spirit" of utilitarian ethics. Together, Kantian **deontology**, utilitarian **consequentialism**, and Humean/Smithian **sentimentalism** frame most secular ethical debate today. All are public-domain texts originally written in English or translated before 1930.

## Candidate passages

Text was copied from the fetched files. Line breaks were joined. Curly apostrophes and quotation marks may have been typed as straight ones.

### 1. Kant, _Groundwork_ I (Ak 4:393–394), the good will

> Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will. [...] Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself.

- **Translation:** T. K. Abbott, PD. "[...]" is my elision of two paragraphs between the opening sentence and the jewel image.
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5682/pg5682.txt
- **Why:** The light imagery ("shine by its own light") and the inner worth of a good will make a natural bridge to John 3:21 ("that his works may be revealed"). Either half works alone.

### 2. Kant, _Groundwork_ II (Ak 4:421), the categorical imperative

> There is therefore but one categorical imperative, namely, this: Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law.

- **Translation:** T. K. Abbott, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5682/pg5682.txt
- **Why:** This is the canonical formula and the Enlightenment's rigorous cousin of the golden rule.

### 3. Kant, _Groundwork_ II (Ak 4:429), the formula of humanity

> So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only.

- **Translation:** T. K. Abbott, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5682/pg5682.txt
- **Why:** It is probably the most influential single sentence in modern human-rights thinking, and short.

### 4. Kant, _Critique of Practical Reason_, Conclusion (Ak 5:161)

> Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within. I have not to search for them and conjecture them as though they were veiled in darkness or were in the transcendent region beyond my horizon; I see them before me and connect them directly with the consciousness of my existence.

- **Translation:** T. K. Abbott, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5683/pg5683.txt
- **Why:** This is the most quoted Kant line, and here it comes with its light/darkness sequel ("not veiled in darkness"). It is also engraved on Kant's tomb plaque in Kaliningrad.

### 5. Mill, _Utilitarianism_ ch. 2, the Greatest Happiness Principle

> The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness.

- **Translation:** original English (1863), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11224/pg11224.txt
- **Why:** It is the definitional sentence of utilitarianism. It is more informative than inspiring, so pair it with #7.

### 6. Mill, _Utilitarianism_ ch. 2, higher pleasures

> It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. And if the fool, or the pig, is of a different opinion, it is because they only know their own side of the question. The other party to the comparison knows both sides.

- **Translation:** original English (1863), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11224/pg11224.txt
- **Why:** It is famous, witty, and self-contained, and it bridges to Socrates (Greek dossier) and Epicurus (_Letter to Menoeceus_ 131–132).

### 7. Mill, _Utilitarianism_ ch. 2, the golden rule

> In the golden rule of Jesus of Nazareth, we read the complete spirit of the ethics of utility. To do as one would be done by, and to love one's neighbour as oneself, constitute the ideal perfection of utilitarian morality.

- **Translation:** original English (1863), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11224/pg11224.txt
- **Why:** It is an explicit secular endorsement of the golden rule, which makes it an ideal bridge to the Christianity, rabbinic, and Islam golden-rule passages.

### 8. Hume, _Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals_ IX.1

> But these principles, we must remark, are social and universal; they form, in a manner, the PARTY of humankind against vice or disorder, its common enemy.

- **Translation:** original English, PD (capitals as printed)
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4320/pg4320.txt
- **Why:** "The party of humankind" is a memorable phrase for universal moral sentiment.

### 9. Hume, _Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals_ IX.2 (the "sensible knave")

> But in all ingenuous natures, the antipathy to treachery and roguery is too strong to be counter-balanced by any views of profit or pecuniary advantage. Inward peace of mind, consciousness of integrity, a satisfactory review of our own conduct; these are circumstances, very requisite to happiness, and will be cherished and cultivated by every honest man, who feels the importance of them.

- **Translation:** original English, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4320/pg4320.txt
- **Why:** This is Hume's answer to the Ring of Gyges, the knave who cheats "with moderation and secrecy". "A satisfactory review of our own conduct" echoes John 3:21 and Seneca _Ep._ 43.

### 10. Adam Smith, _Theory of Moral Sentiments_ I.i.1.1 (opening sentence)

> How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it.

- **Translation:** original English, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67363/pg67363.txt
- **Why:** It is a famous opening that corrects the "selfish Adam Smith" caricature in one sentence.

### 11. Adam Smith, _Theory of Moral Sentiments_ I.i.5.5 (Glasgow numbering)

> And hence it is, that to feel much for others and little for ourselves, that to restrain our selfish, and to indulge our benevolent affections, constitutes the perfection of human nature; and can alone produce among mankind that harmony of sentiments and passions in which consist their whole grace and propriety. As to love our neighbour as we love ourselves is the great law of christianity, so it is the great precept of nature to love ourselves only as we love our neighbour, or what comes to the same thing, as our neighbour is capable of loving us.

- **Translation:** original English (1777 Dublin printing), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67363/pg67363.txt
- **Why:** This is a clever inversion of Leviticus 19:18 / Mark 12:31 ("love ourselves only as we love our neighbour"), and a superb bridge passage.

### 12. Adam Smith, _Theory of Moral Sentiments_ III.3.4 (Glasgow numbering; Part III ch. 2 in the 1777 printing)

> It is not the soft power of humanity, it is not that feeble spark of benevolence which Nature has lighted up in the human heart, that is thus capable of counteracting the strongest impulses of self-love. It is a stronger power, a more forcible motive, which exerts itself upon such occasions. It is reason, principle, conscience, the inhabitant of the breast, the man within, the great judge and arbiter of our conduct.

- **Translation:** original English (1777 Dublin printing), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67363/pg67363.txt
- **Why:** This is the "impartial spectator" as conscience, the inner witness. It parallels Seneca's indwelling spirit that "marks our good and bad deeds" (_Ep._ 41) and Epictetus's "God is within" (_Disc._ I.14).

**Also verified (alternates):**

- Kant, _Groundwork_ II, footnote at Ak 4:430 (Abbott, Gutenberg #5682). This is Kant's **critique of the golden rule** and a great "tension" bridge: "Let it not be thought that the common "quod tibi non vis fieri, etc." could serve here as the rule or principle. For it is only a deduction from the former, though with several limitations; it cannot be a universal law, for it does not contain the principle of duties to oneself, nor of the duties of benevolence to others [...] nor finally that of duties of strict obligation to one another, for on this principle the criminal might argue against the judge who punishes him, and so on."
- Hume, _EPM_ II.1 (Gutenberg #4320): "The epithets SOCIABLE, GOOD-NATURED, HUMANE, MERCIFUL, GRATEFUL, FRIENDLY, GENEROUS, BENEFICENT, or their equivalents, are known in all languages, and universally express the highest merit, which HUMAN NATURE is capable of attaining."

## Beware (common misattributions)

- **"All that is necessary for the triumph of evil is that good men do nothing."** It is often given to Burke or Mill, but it has no source in Burke. Mill did say something related in his 1867 St Andrews inaugural address ("Bad men need nothing more to compass their ends, than that good men should look on and do nothing"). Verify before use.
- **"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions."** This is genuine Hume, but it comes from the _**Treatise**_ (2.3.3), not the _Enquiry_. Cite correctly.
- **"Science is organized knowledge. Wisdom is organized life."** This is attributed to Kant without a source. The first half is Herbert Spencer's.
- **"Act in such a way that you treat humanity..."** This and other modern wordings are copyrighted translations (Gregor, Paton, Ellington). On the site, use Abbott (PD) as in #3.
- **The "invisible hand" as Smith's moral philosophy.** The phrase appears once each in _TMS_ (IV.1.10) and _Wealth of Nations_ (IV.2.9). It is not a slogan for self-interest as a virtue, which is a common misreading.

## Bridges

1. **Golden rule: endorsement versus critique. Mill ch. 2 (#7) and Smith I.i.5.5 (#11) ↔ Kant's footnote at 4:430 ↔ Christianity, Matthew 7:12; Rabbinic, Hillel (b. Shabbat 31a); Confucianism, _Analects_ 15.24.** Mill embraces the golden rule as utilitarianism's "complete spirit". Smith turns it around ("love ourselves only as we love our neighbour"). Kant demotes it as a derivative, flawed rule. This three-way disagreement inside one tradition is a very rich caption.
2. **Inner light and inner witness. Kant's "moral law within" not "veiled in darkness" (#4); Smith's "inhabitant of the breast" (#12) ↔ Stoicism, Seneca _Ep._ 41 and Epictetus _Disc._ I.14; Christianity, John 3:19–21 and Romans 2:15 (law written on the heart); Islam, the hadith "consult your heart" (see the islam dossier).**
3. **The unseen deed. Hume's sensible knave (#9) ↔ Greek, Ring of Gyges (_Republic_ II); Epicureanism, PD 35; Christianity, John 3:20.** Hume admits he cannot refute the knave by argument and appeals instead to "inward peace" and "a satisfactory review of our own conduct". That is close to John's doer of truth who is glad for his works to be seen.
4. **Persons as ends. Kant 4:429 (#3) ↔ Hebrew Bible, Genesis 1:27 (_imago Dei_); Islam, Qur'an 17:70 (God has honoured the children of Adam).** These are secular and theological groundings of human dignity, with similar conclusions.
5. **Higher pleasures. Mill's Socrates dissatisfied (#6) ↔ Epicureanism, _Letter to Menoeceus_ 131–132 ("sober reasoning"); Greek, _Apology_ 38a.** Mill explicitly defends Epicurus against the charge of swinishness in the same chapter.
