# Confucianism (儒家 Rujia, "the school of the scholars")

## Overview

Confucian ethics starts from the conviction that a good life and a good society are made, not found: they are cultivated through learning, ritual practice and the steady correction of one's own faults. The central virtue is **仁 ren** (Legge: "perfect virtue", "benevolence"; modern: "humaneness"), the full flowering of what it is to be human toward others. It is expressed through **禮 li** (ritual propriety, the forms of respectful conduct), guided by **義 yi** (rightness, what is fitting), and anchored in **孝 xiao** (filial piety) and **忠 zhong / 恕 shu** (loyalty to one's own best self, and the reciprocity of "measuring others by oneself"). The ideal person is the **君子 junzi** (Legge: "the superior man"; literally "a ruler's son", re-moralised by Confucius to mean a person noble in character rather than birth), contrasted with the **小人 xiaoren** ("small" or "mean" person).

The later classics push the vision inward. The _Great Learning_ (大學 Daxue) roots world peace in a chain that begins with "making the thoughts sincere" (誠意 cheng yi), and the _Doctrine of the Mean_ (中庸 Zhongyong) makes **誠 cheng**, sincerity or integrity, a cosmic principle: "Sincerity is the way of Heaven." Both texts teach **慎獨 shen du**, "being watchful over oneself when alone", because "there is nothing more visible than what is secret". This is the closest Chinese analogue to John 3:19-21: the self that hides its deeds versus the self whose inner and outer are one, and the conviction that what is hidden will be manifest. Mencius (孟子 Mengzi, c. 372-289 BCE) argued that human nature (性 xing) is good, possessing four "sprouts" (端 duan) of virtue; Xunzi (荀子, c. 310-c. 235 BCE) countered that it is bad and goodness is the product of deliberate effort (偽 wei) and teaching.

Historically, Confucius (孔子 Kongzi, trad. 551-479 BCE) was a minor official and teacher in the state of Lu during the disintegration of the Zhou order. His sayings were compiled by disciples over generations into the _Analects_ (論語 Lunyu). The _Great Learning_ and _Doctrine of the Mean_ are chapters of the _Book of Rites_ (禮記 Liji), elevated by Zhu Xi (朱熹, 1130-1200) into the "Four Books" alongside the _Analects_ and _Mencius_; that canon was the basis of the imperial examinations from 1313 to 1905. Confucianism remains a living ethical and civic tradition across East Asia, though it is less often a separate "religion" than a moral framework many people hold alongside Buddhism, Daoism, or Christianity.

## Candidate passages

### 1. Analects 15.23: the "one word" for a whole life (reciprocity)

- **Translation:** Legge (1861/1893), via Gutenberg #4094: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4094/pg4094.txt
- **Text:** "Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?' The Master said, 'Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.'"
- **Chinese:** 子貢問曰、有一言、而可以終身行之者乎。子曰、其恕乎、己所不欲、勿施於人。
- **Why:** The negative golden rule in its most famous form. It pairs directly with Matthew 7:12 and Hillel. (Numbered 15.24 in some modern editions.)

### 2. Analects 6.28: the positive form

- **Translation:** Legge, Gutenberg #4094 (same URL)
- **Text:** "Now the man of perfect virtue, wishing to be established himself, seeks also to establish others; wishing to be enlarged himself, he seeks also to enlarge others. To be able to judge of others by what is nigh in ourselves;-- this may be called the art of virtue."
- **Chinese:** 夫仁者、己欲立而立人、己欲達而達人。能近取譬、可謂仁之方也已。
- **Why:** This answers the claim that Confucius only had a "negative" golden rule. It is the active, outward-reaching counterpart to 15.23. (6.30 in some numberings.)

### 3. Analects 19.21: faults like eclipses of the sun and moon

- **Translation:** Legge, Gutenberg #4094
- **Text:** "Tsze-kung said, 'The faults of the superior man are like the eclipses of the sun and moon. He has his faults, and all men see them; he changes again, and all men look up to him.'"
- **Chinese:** 子貢曰、君子之過也、如日月之食焉、過也、人皆見之、更也、人皆仰之。
- **Why:** This is the best light-image in the Analects for pairing with John 3:21. The good person does not hide from the light. His faults are as public as an eclipse, and so is his correction.

### 4. Analects 4.8: hearing the Way

- **Translation:** Legge, Gutenberg #4094
- **Text:** "The Master said, 'If a man in the morning hear the right way, he may die in the evening without regret.'"
- **Chinese:** 子曰、朝聞道、夕死、可矣。
- **Why:** One of the most quoted lines in East Asia. It is short and stark, and it places truth above life itself. It bridges to the death theme.

### 5. Analects 14.36: recompense injury with justice

- **Translation:** Legge, Gutenberg #4094
- **Text:** "Some one said, 'What do you say concerning the principle that injury should be recompensed with kindness?' The Master said, 'With what then will you recompense kindness? Recompense injury with justice, and recompense kindness with kindness.'"
- **Chinese:** 或曰、以德報怨、何如。子曰、何以報德。以直報怨、以德報德。
- **Why:** This is a deliberate _tension_ passage. The rejected maxim 以德報怨 appears in _Daodejing_ 63, and Jesus' "love your enemies" goes further still. Good for a thoughtful pairing.

### 6. Great Learning, commentary ch. 6: sincerity and the one who hides

- **Translation:** Legge (1861/1893), Wikisource transcription: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Chinese_Classics/Volume_1/The_Great_Learning (raw: `?action=raw`). Cross-checked against the archive.org scan (OCR p. 324), which reads "Tsang" for "Zeng".
- **Text:** "What is meant by "making the thoughts sincere" is the allowing no self-deception, as when we hate a bad smell, and as when we love what is beautiful. This is called self-enjoyment. Therefore, the superior man must be watchful over himself when he is alone. There is no evil to which the mean man, dwelling retired, will not proceed, but when he sees a superior man, he instantly tries to disguise himself, concealing his evil, and displaying what is good. The other beholds him, as if he saw his heart and reins; of what use is his disguise? This is an instance of the saying "What truly is within will be manifested without." Therefore, the superior man must be watchful over himself when he is alone."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/禮記/大學): 所謂誠其意者，毋自欺也。如惡惡臭，如好好色，此之謂自謙。故君子必慎其獨也。小人閒居爲不善，無所不至，見君子而後厭然，揜其不善而著其善。人之視己，如見其肺肝然，則何益矣？此謂誠於中，形於外，故君子必慎其獨也。
- **Why:** This is the **strongest resonance with John 3:20** in the whole tradition: the wrongdoer conceals his evil when seen, but "what truly is within will be manifested without". It is long, so for display it could be trimmed to the sentences from "There is no evil…" to "…manifested without."

### 7. Doctrine of the Mean 1.3: nothing more visible than what is secret

- **Translation:** Legge, archive.org OCR of _The Four Books_ (Commercial Press reprint): https://archive.org/download/fourbooksconfuci00leggiala/fourbooksconfuci00leggiala_djvu.txt (details: https://archive.org/details/fourbooksconfuci00leggiala)
- **Text:** "There is nothing more visible than what is secret, and nothing more manifest than what is minute. Therefore the superior man is watchful over himself, when he is alone."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/禮記/中庸): 莫見乎隱，莫顯乎微，故君子慎其獨也。
- **Why:** A compact, quotable version of passage 6. It echoes Luke 8:17 ("nothing is secret, that shall not be made manifest") and the logic of John 3:20-21.

### 8. Doctrine of the Mean 21: sincerity and enlightenment (誠 and 明)

- **Translation:** Legge, archive.org OCR (same URL). [OCR "jbhis" corrected to "this"]
- **Text:** "When we have intelligence resulting from sincerity, this condition is to be ascribed to nature; when we have sincerity resulting from intelligence, this condition is to be ascribed to instruction. But given the sincerity, and there shall be the intelligence; given the intelligence, and there shall be the sincerity."
- **Chinese:** 自誠明，謂之性。自明誠，謂之教。誠則明矣，明則誠矣。
- **Why:** 明 ming, which Legge renders "intelligence", literally means _bright, clear, illumined_. A more literal gloss is "from sincerity comes illumination; from illumination, sincerity." This is the Confucian version of "he that doeth truth cometh to the light." Worth a translator's note if displayed.

### 9. Mencius 2A6: the child at the well

- **Translation:** Legge, archive.org OCR of _The Four Books_ (same URL)
- **Text:** "When I say that all men have a mind which cannot bear to see the sufferings of others, my meaning may be illustrated thus: — even nowadays, if men suddenly see a child about to fall into a well, they will without exception experience a feeling of alarm and distress. They will feel so, not as a ground on which they may gain the favor of the child's parents, nor as a ground on which they may seek the praise of their neighbors and friends, nor from a dislike to the reputations of having been unmoved by such a thing."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/孟子/公孫丑上): 今人乍見孺子將入於井，皆有怵惕惻隱之心；非所以內交於孺子之父母也，非所以要譽於鄉黨朋友也，非惡其聲而然也。
- **Why:** The most famous thought-experiment in Chinese ethics, and the core of the claim that human nature is good. The same chapter continues: "The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence…" (惻隱之心，仁之端也).

### 10. Mencius 6A11: seeking the lost heart

- **Translation:** Legge, archive.org OCR (same URL)
- **Text:** "Benevolence is man's mind, and righteousness is man's path. How lamentable is it to neglect the path and not pursue it, to lose this mind and not know to seek it again! When men's fowls and dogs are lost, they know to seek for them again, but they lose their mind, and do not know to seek for it. The great end of learning is nothing else but to seek for the lost mind."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/孟子/告子上): 仁，人心也。義，人路也。舍其路而弗由，放其心而不知求。哀哉！人有雞犬放，則知求之；有放心，而不知求。學問之道無他，求其放心而已矣。
- **Why:** A homely parable that parallels the lost sheep and lost coin of Luke 15. Here what is lost is one's own heart.

### 11. Mencius 7A4: all things complete within

- **Translation:** Legge, archive.org OCR (same URL)
- **Text:** "Mencius said, "All things are already complete in us. There is no greater delight than to be conscious of sincerity on self-examination. If one acts with a vigorous effort at the law of reciprocity, when he seeks for the realization of perfect virtue, nothing can be closer than his approximation to it.""
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/孟子/盡心上): 萬物皆備於我矣，反身而誠，樂莫大焉。強恕而行，求仁莫近焉。
- **Why:** This joins sincerity (誠) and reciprocity (恕) in three sentences, and it makes a good positive counterpart to "he that doeth truth cometh to the light."

### 12. Xunzi, Book 1 (勸學 "An Encouragement to Study"): opening

- **Translation:** Homer H. Dubs (1928), archive.org OCR: https://archive.org/download/in.ernet.dli.2015.280269/2015.280269.Hsuntze-Works_djvu.txt (details: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.280269). Dubs's marginal headings and footnote markers, which the OCR runs into the text, have been removed.
- **Text:** "The superior man says: Study should never stop. Green dye is taken from blue, but it is nearer the colour of nature than is blue. Ice comes from water, but is colder than water."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/荀子/勸學篇): 君子曰：學不可以已。靑，取之於藍而靑於藍；冰，水爲之而寒於水。
- **Why:** This is the famous source of the proverb 青出於藍 ("the student surpasses the master"), and it is the most quotable line from Xunzi. Note that Dubs's "nearer the colour of nature" is an interpretive rendering; the Chinese says simply "bluer than the indigo". Dubs also renders Xunzi's thesis (Book 23, 性惡篇): "The nature of man is evil; his goodness is only acquired training" (人之性惡，其善者僞也). That line is verified in the same file and is a useful tension-pair with Mencius 2A6.

## Bridges

1. **The hidden and the manifest: Christianity (John 3:19-21; Luke 8:17).** _Great Learning_ ch. 6 and _Doctrine of the Mean_ 1.3 share John's diagnosis: the wrongdoer "conceals his evil" when seen, yet "what truly is within will be manifested without." The difference is that Confucian texts locate the light in the eyes of others and the order of things, not in a divine Light coming into the world.
2. **Sincerity → illumination: Shinto and Japanese ethics (誠 makoto).** The _Doctrine of the Mean_'s 誠 is the same character as Japanese _makoto_. Through Neo-Confucian learning it became central to Tokugawa and Meiji ethics; see the bushido file and Nitobe's chapter on "Veracity and Sincerity".
3. **The golden rule: Judaism, Christianity, Mohism.** Analects 15.23 (negative) and 6.28 (positive) can be set beside Hillel (Shabbat 31a) and Matthew 7:12. Mozi pushes further, telling us to "regard the persons of others as one's self" (兼愛中), which Mencius attacked as denying special duties to one's father (_Mencius_ 3B9).
4. **Repaying injury: tension with Daoism and Jesus.** Analects 14.36 explicitly rejects 以德報怨, "recompense injury with kindness", which _Daodejing_ 63 endorses (報怨以德) and which Matthew 5:44 radicalises into "love your enemies". This makes a rich three-way contrast about justice and grace.
5. **Death and truth: Socrates / Buddhism.** Analects 4.8 ("hear the right way in the morning, die in the evening without regret") sits naturally beside Socrates in the _Apology_, who chose death over abandoning philosophy, and beside _Dhammapada_ 113 (Max Müller 1881, Gutenberg #2017): "And he who lives a hundred years, not seeing beginning and end, a life of one day is better if a man sees beginning and end."
6. **Human nature: Mencius vs. Xunzi vs. Augustine.** The child-at-the-well (good nature) and Xunzi's "nature is evil" mirror Pelagius vs. Augustine. A comparative study exists on exactly this pairing: Aaron Stalnaker, _Overcoming Our Evil_ (2006).

## Beware (common misattributions)

- **"Choose a job you love, and you will never have to work a day in your life."** Not in any Confucian text; it is modern.
- **"Better to light a candle than to curse the darkness."** Widely tagged "Confucius" or "Chinese proverb", but no classical source is known. It was popularised in the 20th century, notably as the motto of the Christophers (founded 1945), and a 1907 sermon by William L. Watkinson is often cited as the earliest print use. _Highly tempting for this page's light theme. Do not attribute it to Confucius._
- **"It does not matter how slowly you go as long as you do not stop."** Not a verified Analects saying, at best a loose modern paraphrase. Analects 9.18 (the mound of earth, 譬如為山) expresses a related idea; check it if used.
- **"Before you embark on a journey of revenge, dig two graves."** Not in the classics; this is a modern attribution.
- **"Real knowledge is to know the extent of one's ignorance."** A loose paraphrase of Analects 2.17. Use Legge's actual wording: "When you know a thing, to hold that you know it; and when you do not know a thing, to allow that you do not know it;-- this is knowledge." (Verified in Gutenberg #4094.)
- **"Confucius say…"** jokes are 20th-century American humour.
- Do not attribute the _Great Learning_ or the _Doctrine of the Mean_ to Confucius himself. They are traditionally ascribed to his disciple Zengzi and his grandson Zisi (子思) respectively, and are probably later compilations.
