# Epicureanism

## Overview

**Epicurus** (341–270 BCE) founded his school in a house and garden just outside Athens around 306 BCE. It became known simply as "the Garden" (Κῆπος, _Kēpos_). Unusually for the time, its members included women and slaves. Epicurus's ethics is hedonist, but not in the popular sense. The goal of life is **pleasure** (ἡδονή, _hēdonē_), which he defines chiefly as the _absence_ of pain: freedom from bodily pain (ἀπονία, _aponia_) and from mental disturbance (ἀταραξία, _ataraxia_, "untroubledness"). The greatest obstacles to that tranquillity are false beliefs. These are fear of the gods, fear of death, and "empty" desires for wealth, fame, and power. His cure is the **tetrapharmakos**, the "fourfold remedy" later summarised by Philodemus: God is not to be feared; death is nothing to fear; the good is easy to obtain; the terrible is easy to endure.

The remedies rest on a physics. Epicurus followed Democritus in holding that everything is made of atoms moving in the void. The soul is made of fine atoms that disperse at death, so "death is nothing to us." The gods exist, but they live in blessed tranquillity and neither reward nor punish. Desires are sorted into natural and necessary ones (food, shelter, friendship), natural but unnecessary ones (luxury), and those that are neither, being "empty" or groundless (κεναί, _kenai_), such as desires for statues and crowns. **Prudence** (φρόνησις, _phronēsis_) is the greatest good because it does the sorting. Epicurus insists that pleasure and virtue are inseparable: "it is impossible to live a pleasant life without living wisely and well and justly." Justice is a compact (συνθήκη, _synthēkē_) "neither to harm nor be harmed." **Friendship** (φιλία, _philia_) is the crowning good of the sage's life, and the school itself was a community of friends. Its motto was "live unnoticed" (λάθε βιώσας, _lathe biōsas_).

Most of Epicurus's vast output is lost. The surviving ethical core comes from three sources. Diogenes Laertius, _Lives of Eminent Philosophers_ Book X (3rd century CE), preserves three letters and the forty _Principal Doctrines_ (Κύριαι Δόξαι, _Kyriai Doxai_). The _Vatican Sayings_ were found in a 14th-century Vatican manuscript in 1888. The Roman poet **Lucretius** (c. 99–55 BCE) wrote the great Latin poem _De Rerum Natura_ ("On the Nature of Things"). Christian writers attacked Epicureanism for centuries as atheist and sensualist. Its rediscovery, especially Poggio Bracciolini's recovery of Lucretius in 1417, fed into early modern science and Enlightenment thought.

## Candidate passages

Text was copied from the fetched pages. Line breaks were joined and section numbers were removed from the running text. Curly apostrophes and quotation marks may have been typed as straight ones.

### 1. _Letter to Menoeceus_ 122 (Diogenes Laertius X.122)

> Let no one be slow to seek wisdom when he is young nor weary in the search thereof when he is grown old. For no age is too early or too late for the health of the soul.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** It is a warm, universal opening line. "Health of the soul" shows philosophy as medicine.

### 2. _Letter to Menoeceus_ 124–125 (D.L. X.124–125)

> Accustom thyself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply sentience, and death is the privation of all sentience [...] Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US. "[...]" is my elision.
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** This is the most famous Epicurean argument and a natural anchor for the "death" theme. The second sentence works on its own.

### 3. _Letter to Menoeceus_ 131–132 (D.L. X.131–132)

> By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul. It is not an unbroken succession of drinking-bouts and of revelry, not sexual love, not the enjoyment of the fish and other delicacies of a luxurious table, which produce a pleasant life; it is sober reasoning, searching out the grounds of every choice and avoidance, and banishing those beliefs through which the greatest tumults take possession of the soul.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** Epicurus corrects the "epicure" caricature himself. This is an essential context-setter.

### 4. _Principal Doctrines_ 5

> It is impossible to live a pleasant life without living wisely and well and justly, and it is impossible to live wisely and well and justly without living pleasantly.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** It ties pleasure and virtue together in one sentence.

### 5. _Principal Doctrines_ 17

> The just man enjoys the greatest peace of mind, while the unjust is full of the utmost disquietude.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** It is short and stands alone. It is the Epicurean counterpart to the Psalms' and Proverbs' "the wicked have no peace" theme.

### 6. _Principal Doctrines_ 27

> Of all the means which are procured by wisdom to ensure happiness throughout the whole of life, by far the most important is the acquisition of friends.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** Friendship is at the centre of the good life. It pairs with Aristotle _NE_ VIII.1.

### 7. _Principal Doctrines_ 35

> It is impossible for the man who secretly violates any article of the social compact to feel confident that he will remain undiscovered, even if he has already escaped ten thousand times; for right on to the end of his life he is never sure he will not be detected.

- **Translation:** R. D. Hicks (1925), PD in the US
- **Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_X
- **Why:** This is the Epicurean answer to the Ring of Gyges and a fascinating counterpoint to John 3:20 ("lest his works would be exposed"). Hidden deeds bring endless anxiety. Note that the motive here is prudential (fear of discovery), not love of the light. That tension is worth a caption.

### 8. _Vatican Saying_ 14

> We are born only once and cannot be born twice, and must forever live no more. You don't control tomorrow, yet you postpone joy. Life is ruined by putting things off, and each of us dies without truly living.

- **Translation:** Peter Saint-Andre (2010), **CC0**
- **Source URL:** https://www.monadnock.net/epicurus/vatican-sayings.html
- **Why:** It is urgent, modern-sounding, and about death and joy. It echoes Seneca _Ep._ 1 ("While we are postponing, life speeds by").

### 9. _Vatican Saying_ 52

> Friendship dances around the world, announcing to each of us that we must awaken to happiness.

- **Translation:** Peter Saint-Andre (2010), **CC0**
- **Source URL:** https://www.monadnock.net/epicurus/vatican-sayings.html
- **Why:** This is the most lyrical line in the Epicurean corpus, and it is short.

### 10. _Vatican Saying_ 54

> Do not pretend to love and practice wisdom, but love and practice wisdom in reality; for we need not the appearance of health but true health.

- **Translation:** Peter Saint-Andre (2010), **CC0**
- **Source URL:** https://www.monadnock.net/epicurus/vatican-sayings.html
- **Why:** It sets appearance against reality, close to James 1:22 ("doers of the word, not only hearers") and to Marcus X.16.

### 11. Lucretius, _De Rerum Natura_ II.75–79 (Latin lines)

> The nations wax, the nations wane away; In a brief space the generations pass, And like to runners hand the lamp of life One unto other.

- **Translation:** William Ellery Leonard (1916), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/785/pg785.txt
- **Why:** Latin: _et quasi cursores vitai lampada tradunt_. It is the famous "torch of life" image, about mortality and continuity, and it fits the light theme. Restore Leonard's verse line breaks if displayed.

### 12. Lucretius, _De Rerum Natura_ III.1–6 (hymn to Epicurus)

> O thou who first uplifted in such dark So clear a torch aloft, who first shed light Upon the profitable ends of man, O thee I follow, glory of the Greeks, And set my footsteps squarely planted now Even in the impress and the marks of thine--

- **Translation:** William Ellery Leonard (1916), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/785/pg785.txt
- **Why:** Latin: _E tenebris tantis tam clarum extollere lumen_. Light out of great darkness is applied to a human teacher, not a god. It is a deliberate and provocative parallel to John 1:5 and 3:19. Restore the verse line breaks when displaying.

**Also verified (alternates):**

- Lucretius III.830–831 (Leonard): "Therefore death to us / Is nothing, nor concerns us in the least, / Since nature of mind is mortal evermore." (Gutenberg #785)
- Lucretius II.1–6 (Leonard), the _suave mari magno_ opening: "'Tis sweet, when, down the mighty main, the winds / Roll up its waste of waters, from the land / To watch another's labouring anguish far, / Not that we joyously delight that man / Should thus be smitten, but because 'tis sweet / To mark what evils we ourselves be spared". This is famous but easily misread as gloating. Use with care.
- _Principal Doctrines_ 15 (Hicks): "Nature's wealth at once has its bounds and is easy to procure; but the wealth of vain fancies recedes to an infinite distance."
- _Vatican Saying_ 7 (Saint-Andre): "It is easy to commit an injustice undetected, but impossible to be sure that you have escaped detection."

## Beware (common misattributions)

- **The "Epicurean paradox" / problem of evil: "Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is not omnipotent..."** This is **not found in any surviving text of Epicurus**. The argument is reported by the Christian writer Lactantius (_De Ira Dei_ 13, c. 313 CE), who ascribes it to Epicurus. The popular wording is essentially Hume's (_Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion_, Part X, 1779). Attribute it to "Lactantius, reporting Epicurus" or to Hume.
- **"I was not; I was; I am not; I do not care"** (_non fui, fui, non sum, non curo_). This is a Roman funerary formula associated with Epicurean sentiment, not a saying of Epicurus.
- **"Eat, drink, and be merry, for tomorrow we die."** This is not Epicurean. It blends Ecclesiastes 8:15, Isaiah 22:13, and 1 Corinthians 15:32, where Paul cites it as the creed of those who deny resurrection. It actually contradicts Epicurus (see #3).
- **"Do not spoil what you have by desiring what you have not..."** This is a loose paraphrase of _Vatican Saying_ 35. Saint-Andre's CC0 wording (verified on the page above) is: "Don't ruin the things you have by wanting what you don't have, but realize that they too are things you once did wish for." Use that wording instead.
- **"Of all the things which wisdom provides to make us entirely happy, much the greatest is friendship."** This is a legitimate _variant translation_ of PD 27, not a fake, but quote one translation consistently.

## Bridges

1. **Hiding versus the light. _Principal Doctrines_ 35 ↔ Christianity, John 3:20; Greek, Ring of Gyges (_Republic_ II); Stoicism, Seneca _Ep._ 43.** All four agree that concealment is no refuge. Epicurus's reason is _anxiety_ (you are never sure), Seneca's is conscience, and John's is the will that loves darkness. This is a strong three-way caption opportunity.
2. **Light from darkness. Lucretius III.1–6 ↔ Christianity, John 1:5 / 3:19; Islam, Qur'an 2:257 (from darkness into light).** The image is identical, but Lucretius's light is natural science dispelling _religious_ fear. That is a real tension with the Abrahamic traditions and deserves an honest caption.
3. **Death. _Letter to Menoeceus_ 125 ↔ Greek, _Apology_ 40c–41d; Stoicism, _Enchiridion_ 5; Hebrew Bible, Ecclesiastes 9:4–10 (no work in Sheol, so enjoy your life now).** Ecclesiastes and Epicurus are close in mood, with carpe diem in the face of death. They differ sharply on God.
4. **Friendship. PD 27, VS 52 ↔ Greek, Aristotle _NE_ VIII.1; Norse, _Hávamál_ friendship stanzas (c. 41–47; numbering varies by edition, see the norse dossier for verified wording).**
5. **Contentment and simple needs. _Letter to Menoeceus_ 130–131 ("bread and water") ↔ Hebrew Bible, Proverbs 30:8–9 (give me neither poverty nor riches); Christianity, Philippians 4:11–12; Daoism, _Daodejing_ 33/44 (knowing what is enough).**
6. **Justice as compact. PD 31–33 ↔ Enlightenment, Hume's _Enquiry_ III (justice from utility and convention).** The contrast is with natural-law traditions (Stoicism, Islam, Thomism) that hold justice to be real independently of agreement.
