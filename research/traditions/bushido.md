# Japanese ethics: Shōtoku's Constitution, Shinto sincerity, Bushidō, Hagakure

## Overview

This entry gathers several strata of Japanese moral thought, and they should not be blended into one timeless "samurai code". The oldest native layer is Shinto (神道), which has almost no written moral law. Its ethic is one of **purity and brightness of heart**: _kiyoki akaki kokoro_ (清き明き心, "a pure, bright heart") and later **makoto** (誠, sincerity/truthfulness). The opposite is _kitanaki kokoro_ (穢き心), a defiled or dark heart, cleansed by _harae_ (祓, purification). A sincere heart is "bright" and a deceitful one "dark", which gives Japanese ethics a natural point of contact with John 3:19–21. Onto this ground came Confucian and Buddhist ethics from the continent. The **Seventeen-Article Constitution** (_Jūshichijō kenpō_, 十七条憲法), attributed to Prince Shōtoku (聖徳太子, 574–622) and dated 604 in the _Nihon shoki_ (日本書紀, compiled 720), is a homily to officials. It weaves together harmony (和 _wa_, from the Analects), reverence for the Buddhist "three treasures", good faith (信 _shin_), and an unusual Buddhist humility about one's own rightness (Article X: "we are all ordinary men", 共是凡夫耳). Many historians now doubt Shōtoku's personal authorship and some date the text later, so present it as "attributed to".

**Bushidō** (武士道, "the way of the warrior") names an ethos of the samurai class: loyalty (忠義 _chūgi_), rectitude (義 _gi_), courage (勇 _yū_), benevolence (仁 _jin_), propriety (礼 _rei_), sincerity (誠 _makoto_) and honor (名誉 _meiyo_). These were shaped by Zen, Confucianism (especially Wang Yangming's 陽明学 _Yōmeigaku_, "knowing and acting are one") and Shinto. Historians stress that Bushidō as a _unified national code_ is largely a **Meiji-era (1868–1912) construction**. Inazō Nitobe's English-language _Bushido: The Soul of Japan_ (1899) presented it to the West as Japan's counterpart of chivalry. Nitobe was a Quaker and an internationalist, and his book is idealizing, apologetic, and full of Christian parallels. The concept was then pressed into service by state nationalism and militarism up to 1945.

The **Hagakure** (葉隠, "Hidden by Leaves") is a different kind of text. It records the sayings of Yamamoto Tsunetomo (山本常朝, 1659–1719), a retainer of the Saga domain who had become a monk, taken down by Tashiro Tsuramoto c. 1709–1716. It circulated locally in manuscript. Its famous line "the way of the warrior is found in dying" (武士道と云ふは死ぬ事と見付けたり) was promoted in the 1930s–40s and used to romanticize suicide attacks. Postwar readers (including Mishima Yukio) reread it in yet other ways. Its core can be read as a _memento mori_ discipline: living each day as one already dead, so as to act without fear or self-interest. This is a legitimate and serious theme, but it should be framed with care on a public page.

## Candidate passages

### 1. Seventeen-Article Constitution, Article I: Harmony

- **Citation:** _Nihongi_ XXII (Suiko 12 [604], 4th month), Article I.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1896.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Harmony is to be valued, and an avoidance of wanton opposition to be honoured. All men are influenced by class-feelings, and there are few who are intelligent. Hence there are some who disobey their lords and fathers, or who maintain feuds with the neighboring villages. But when those above are harmonious and those below are friendly, and there is concord in the discussion of business, right views of things spontaneously gain acceptance. Then what is there which cannot be accomplished!
- **Kanbun:** 一曰：以和爲貴，無忤爲宗。人皆有黨，亦少達者。是以或不順君父，乍違于隣里。然上和下睦，諧於論事，則事理自通，何事不成？
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_XXII ; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/日本書紀/卷第廿二
- **Why:** The single most famous sentence in Japanese political ethics (以和為貴). Note that _wa_ here means concord reached through **discussion** ("concord in the discussion of business"), not silent conformity. The first sentence works alone.

### 2. Article X: Cease from wrath; "we are all ordinary men"

- **Citation:** _Nihongi_ XXII, Article X.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1896.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Let us cease from wrath, and refrain from angry looks. Nor let us be resentful when others differ from us. For all men have hearts, and each heart has its own leanings. Their right is our wrong, and our right is their wrong. We are not unquestionably sages, nor are they unquestionably fools. Both of us are simply ordinary men. How can any one lay down a rule by which to distinguish right from wrong? For we are all, one with another, wise and foolish, like a ring which has no end. Therefore, although others give way to anger, let us on the contrary dread our own faults, and though we alone may be in the right, let us follow the multitude and act like them.
- **Kanbun:** 十曰：絶忿棄瞋，不怒人違。人皆有心，心各有執。彼是則我非，我是則彼非。我必非聖，彼必非愚，共是凡夫耳。是非之理，詎能可定？相共賢愚，如鐶无端。是以彼人雖瞋，還恐我失，我獨雖得，從衆同舉。
- **URL:** as above.
- **Why:** **Probably the strongest candidate in this file.** It is a Buddhist-inflected humility about one's own righteousness (_bonbu_ 凡夫, "ordinary unenlightened person"), with a vivid image (a ring without end). "We are not unquestionably sages, nor are they unquestionably fools" stands on its own. Caveat: the final clause ("follow the multitude") can read as conformism, so consider ending the excerpt at "dread our own faults".

### 3. Article IX: Good faith is the foundation of right

- **Citation:** _Nihongi_ XXII, Article IX.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1896.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Good faith is the foundation of right. In everything let there be good faith, for in it there surely consists the good and the bad, success and failure. If the lord and the vassal observe good faith one with another, what is there which cannot be accomplished? If the lord and the vassal do not observe good faith towards one another, everything without exception ends in failure.
- **Kanbun:** 九曰：信是義本，毎事有信。其善惡成敗，要在于信。羣臣共信，何事不成？羣臣無信，萬事悉敗。
- **URL:** as above.
- **Why:** 信是義本, "trustworthiness is the root of righteousness", is the sincerity theme in its civic form. (Note: the kanbun has 羣臣, "the ministers", where Aston writes "lord and vassal"; the yomikudashi on ja.wikisource reads 君臣.)

### 4. Article VI: Conceal not the good; correct the wrong

- **Citation:** _Nihongi_ XXII, Article VI.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1896.
- **Text (verbatim; first three sentences):**
  > Chastise that which is evil and encourage that which is good. This was the excellent rule of antiquity. Conceal not, therefore, the good qualities of others, and fail not to correct that which is wrong when you see it.
- **Kanbun:** 六曰：懲惡勸善，古之良典。是以無匿人善，見惡必匡。
- **URL:** as above.
- **Why:** This is about bringing things into the open, both the good and the wrong (無匿人善, "do not hide others' goodness"). It is a quiet echo of John 3:21's "made manifest". The remainder of the article attacks flatterers and deceivers.

### 5. Article XVII: Decide important matters together

- **Citation:** _Nihongi_ XXII, Article XVII.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1896.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Decisions on important matters should not be made by one person alone. They should be discussed with many. But small matters are of less consequence. It is unnecessary to consult a number of people. It is only in the case of the discussion of weighty affairs, when there is a suspicion that they may miscarry, that one should arrange matters in concert with others, so as to arrive at the right conclusion.
- **Kanbun:** 十七曰：夫事不可獨断，必與衆宜論。少事是輕，不可必衆。唯逯論大事，若疑有失，故與衆相辨，辭則得理。
- **URL:** as above.
- **Why:** Humility of judgment as an institution. It is often cited as an early Japanese ideal of deliberation. The first two sentences are quotable.

### 6. Nitobe, "Veracity or Truthfulness": sincerity as the beginning and end of things

- **Citation:** Nitobe, _Bushido: The Soul of Japan_, chapter "Veracity or Truthfulness".
- **Translation:** Nitobe's own English (13th ed., 1908).
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > "Propriety carried beyond right bounds," says Masamuné, "becomes a lie." An ancient poet has outdone Polonius in the advice he gives: "To thyself be faithful: if in thy heart thou strayest not from truth, without prayer of thine the Gods will keep thee whole." The apotheosis of Sincerity to which Tsu-tsu gives expression in the _Doctrine of the Mean_, attributes to it transcendental powers, almost identifying them with the Divine. "Sincerity is the end and the beginning of all things; without Sincerity there would be nothing."
- **Japanese original of the poem (not in Nitobe; from ja.wikipedia, 菅原道真):** 心だに 誠の道にかなひなば 祈らずとても 神や守らん
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12096/pg12096.txt ; https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/菅原道真
- **Why:** This is the classic **makoto** text. The "ancient poet" is traditionally Sugawara no Michizane, but the attribution is doubtful (see Beware). Its message, that a heart that keeps to truth needs no further prayer, parallels "he that doeth truth cometh to the light". The _Doctrine of the Mean_ line (誠者物之終始，不誠無物, ch. 25) links to the Confucianism file.

### 7. Nitobe, "Sources of Bushido": the light of conscience (Miwa Shissai, after Wang Yangming)

- **Citation:** Nitobe, chapter "Sources of Bushido". Nitobe's footnote 7 identifies the "Japanese disciple" as Miwa Shissai (三輪執斎, 1669–1744).
- **Translation:** Nitobe's English, 1908.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > "The lord of heaven and earth, of all living beings, dwelling in the heart of man, becomes his mind (_Kokoro_); hence a mind is a living thing, and is ever luminous:" and again, "The spiritual light of our essential being is pure, and is not affected by the will of man. Spontaneously springing up in our mind, it shows what is right and wrong: it is then called conscience; it is even the light that proceedeth from the god of heaven."
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12096/pg12096.txt
- **Why:** An explicitly **light-centered** account of conscience (Wang Yangming's 良知 _ryōchi_, "innate knowing of the good"). The light "shows what is right and wrong", which is close to John 3:20–21, where the light reproves deeds. Nitobe himself compares it to Quaker mysticism (Isaac Penington).

### 8. Nitobe, "Honor": "return not anger" (Ogawa, Kumazawa, Saigō)

- **Citation:** Nitobe, chapter "Honor". Kumazawa Banzan (熊沢蕃山, 1619–1691) and Saigō Takamori (西郷隆盛, 1828–1877). Nitobe does not further identify "Ogawa".
- **Translation:** Nitobe's English, 1908.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Take, for instance, this saying of Ogawa: "When others speak all manner of evil things against thee, return not evil for evil, but rather reflect that thou wast not more faithful in the discharge of thy duties." Take another of Kumazawa:—"When others blame thee, blame them not; when others are angry at thee, return not anger. Joy cometh only as Passion and Desire part."
- **Further, Saigō (verbatim):**
  > "The Way is the way of Heaven and Earth: Man's place is to follow it: therefore make it the object of thy life to reverence Heaven. Heaven loves me and others with equal love; therefore with the love wherewith thou lovest thyself, love others. Make not Man thy partner but Heaven, and making Heaven thy partner do thy best. Never condemn others; but see to it that thou comest not short of thine own mark."
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12096/pg12096.txt
- **Why:** Non-retaliation and a **golden-rule** formulation ("with the love wherewith thou lovest thyself, love others") within the warrior tradition. Nitobe candidly adds that "very few attained this sublime height". Saigō's 敬天愛人 (_keiten aijin_, "revere Heaven, love people") is the underlying motto.

### 9. Nitobe, "Benevolence": _bushi no nasake_, the tenderness of a warrior

- **Citation:** Nitobe, chapter "Benevolence, the Feeling of Distress".
- **Translation:** Nitobe's English, 1908.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > We knew Benevolence was a tender virtue and mother-like. If upright Rectitude and stern Justice were peculiarly masculine, Mercy had the gentleness and the persuasiveness of a feminine nature. [...] Masamuné expressed it well in his oft-quoted aphorism—"Rectitude carried to excess hardens into stiffness; Benevolence indulged beyond measure sinks into weakness."
- **Also (verbatim):**
  > Benevolence to the weak, the downtrodden or the vanquished, was ever extolled as peculiarly becoming to a samurai.
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12096/pg12096.txt
- **Why:** The "Masamuné" (Date Masamune) aphorism is a crisp, balanced maxim. _Bushi no nasake_ (武士の情け) is still a living idiom for mercy shown by the strong.

### 10. Shinto shrine oracles: "even a hair of pride... as it were by a great cloud"

- **Citation:** Oracle of the Gods of Kasuga (春日), as translated in Aston, _Shinto (The Way of the Gods)_, ch. "Morals, Law, and Purity", section "Shinto Oracles". These oracle collections are medieval–Edo; Aston does not date them individually.
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1905.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > Even though men prepare for us a pure abode and offer there the rare things of the land, though they hang up offerings of the seven precious things, and with anxious hearts pray to us for hundreds of days, yet will we refuse to enter the house of the depraved and miserly. But we will surely visit the dwellings even of those in deep mourning without an invitation, if loving-kindness is there always. The reason is that we make loving-kindness our shintai.
  >
  > Hear all men! If you desire to obtain help from the Gods, put away pride. Even a hair of pride shuts you off from the Gods as it were by a great cloud.
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46038/pg46038.txt
- **Why:** Humility as an image of light and cloud. Loving-kindness, not ritual, is the god's "body" (_shintai_, 神体). The gods visit even ritually "unclean" mourners if love is there. The second paragraph is a superb short candidate.

### 11. Shinto oracles: "If that which is within is not bright" (Tatsuta) and Michizane (Temman Tenjin)

- **Citation:** Oracles of Tatsuta (龍田, the Wind-God) and of Temman Tenjin (the deified Sugawara no Michizane), in Aston, _Shinto_, "Shinto Oracles".
- **Translation:** W. G. Aston, 1905.
- **Text (verbatim):**
  > All ye of high and low degree, rather than pray to Heaven-and-Earth, rather than pray to all the Kami, dutifully serve your parents. For your parents are the Gods of without and within. If that which is within is not bright it is useless to pray only for that which is without.
  >
  > All ye who come before me hoping to attain the accomplishment of your desires, pray with hearts pure from falsehood, clean within and without, reflecting the truth like a mirror.
- **URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/46038/pg46038.txt
- **Why:** These are the clearest English expressions of the Shinto ideal of the **bright, unclouded heart** (明き心), and of sincerity as a mirror reflecting truth. (Aston's note says "Gods of without and within" alludes to the Outer and Inner Shrines of Ise.) The last clause of the first oracle and all of the second are short enough to display.

### 12. Hagakure, Kikigaki I.2: "The way of the warrior is found in dying" (Japanese only)

- **Citation:** _Hagakure_ 聞書第一 (Book 1), section 2. Opening sentence: 武士道と云ふは死ぬ事と見付けたり.
- **Text (verbatim, as quoted with an editorial ellipsis in ja.wikipedia, citing 山本博文『殉死の構造』2008, pp. 208–212):**
  > 二つ〳〵の場にて、早く死ぬ方に片付ばかり也。別に子細なし。胸すわつて進む也。（中略）二つ〳〵の場にて、図に当たるやうにする事は及ばざる事也。我人、生る方がすき也。多分すきの方に理が付べし。若図に迦れて生たらば、腰ぬけ也。此境危ふき也。図に迦れて死たらば、気違にて恥にはならず、是は武道の丈夫也。毎朝毎夕、改めては死々、常住死身に成て居る時は、武道に自由を得、一生落度なく、家職を仕課すべき也。
- **Working gloss (mine, NOT a published translation; for the owner's orientation only):** "...Every morning and every evening, die anew; when one lives constantly as one already dead, one gains freedom in the Way, and can fulfil one's calling all one's life without fault."
- **URL:** https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/葉隠
- **Why (and caution):** The final sentence (毎朝毎夕、改めては死々…) is a genuine **death-meditation** passage, comparable to Plato's "practising dying" or to Christian _memento mori_. However, the Hagakure was used to glorify kamikaze and _gyokusai_ deaths, and Japanese Wikipedia itself discusses that misreading. **No PD English exists.** Display only with an original translation and context, or leave it as background.

## Bridges

1. **John 3:19–21 / Matthew 6:22–23 (Christianity).** The Tatsuta oracle's "If that which is within is not bright it is useless to pray only for that which is without" (#11) is close to "if the light that is in thee be darkness, how great is that darkness". Miwa Shissai's luminous conscience, which "shows what is right and wrong" (#7), parallels John 3:20 ("lest his deeds should be reproved"). Article VI's "conceal not" (#4) echoes "that his deeds may be made manifest".
2. **Confucianism.** Article I's 以和爲貴 quotes Analects 1.12 (禮之用，和為貴). Article XVI's 使民以時 ("employ the people at seasonable times") quotes Analects 1.5. Nitobe's sincerity line is _Doctrine of the Mean_ 25 (誠者物之終始，不誠無物). Saigō's 敬天愛人 is Neo-Confucian. Shōtoku's ethics is continental thought naturalized.
3. **Daoism, Zhuangzi ch. 2 ("Qiwulun").** Article X's 彼是則我非，我是則彼非 ("their right is our wrong...") uses the same 是/非, 彼/此 vocabulary as Zhuangzi's critique of fixed right-and-wrong (彼亦一是非，此亦一是非). In Article X, though, the conclusion is moral humility ("dread our own faults") rather than Daoist detachment.
4. **Non-retaliation.** Kumazawa's "when others are angry at thee, return not anger" (#8) parallels Dhammapada 3–5, Shaikh Farid's "do good for evil" (sikhism file, Ang 1381) and Romans 12:17. Saigō's "with the love wherewith thou lovest thyself, love others" is a golden rule close to Leviticus 19:18 and Mark 12:31.
5. **Humility.** The Kasuga oracle's "a hair of pride... as it were by a great cloud" (#10) and Shōtoku's "we are all ordinary men" (#2) parallel Asa ki Var's simmal tree (sikhism file), Daodejing 22 ("He does not show himself, so he shines"; verify against the daoism file) and James 4:6 ("God resisteth the proud").
6. **Death (a tension).** The Hagakure's daily dying (#12) parallels Plato, _Phaedo_ 67e (philosophers "practise dying"), and Montaigne's "to philosophize is to learn to die". It stands in sharp tension with Jain and Buddhist non-violence and with the Gospel's light imagery: the same discipline can free a person from fear, or bind them to a lord's cause unto self-destruction.

## Beware

- **"The seven virtues of Bushidō"** (gi, yū, jin, rei, makoto, meiyo, chūgi) is a modern list derived from Nitobe's chapter titles. No pre-modern code was ever written in that form, and Nitobe himself says Bushidō "is not a written code".
- **Hagakure quotes online** almost always come from **W. S. Wilson's copyrighted 1979 translation**, often via the film _Ghost Dog_. Many are also abridged out of context.
- **"心だに誠の道にかなひなば…"** is popularly attributed to Sugawara no Michizane, but the attribution is traditional and doubtful (it is not in his secure works). Say "attributed to Michizane".
- **Prince Shōtoku's authorship** of the Seventeen Articles is debated, and some scholars date the text later than 604. Use "attributed to Prince Shōtoku (Nihon shoki, 720)".
- **Miyamoto Musashi "quotes"** circulate widely without a source in _Gorin no sho_ or the _Dokkōdō_. Do not use any of them without locating it in a real edition.
- **Do not present _wa_ (和) as "conformity" or as an ethnic essence.** That reading is a modern nationalist gloss. Article I itself grounds harmony in discussion.
- **Do not let the page romanticize death in battle or suicide.** Bushidō rhetoric was used to mobilize the Pacific War, and Nitobe's book (1899) predates and differs from that use.
