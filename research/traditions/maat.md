# Ancient Egyptian Ma'at

## Overview

**Maat** (_mꜣꜥt_, conventionally "Ma'at") is the Egyptian concept that unites truth, justice, right order and cosmic balance. It was personified as a goddess with an ostrich feather, the daughter of the sun-god Ra. Its opposite is **isfet** (_jsft_), meaning disorder, falsehood and violence. The king's first duty was to "do maat and drive out isfet", but every person was expected to "speak maat and do maat" (_ḏd mꜣꜥt, jr mꜣꜥt_). In the formula many scholars quote, maat is a principle of connective justice (Jan Assmann's _konnektive Gerechtigkeit_): acting for one another, reciprocity, remembering benefactors, and the idea that deeds return to the doer. At death the heart (_jb_) is weighed in the balance against the feather of Maat before Osiris. One who passed was declared _mꜣꜥ-ḫrw_, "true of voice" or "justified".

The ethics is carried by three main genres:

1. **Sebayt** (_sbꜣyt_, "instructions" or "teachings"). These are wisdom books in the voice of a father, often a vizier, advising his son. They include _The Instruction of Ptahhotep_ (attributed to a 5th-Dynasty vizier, c. 2400 BCE, but composed in Middle Egyptian in the Middle Kingdom, c. 1900 BCE; best copy Papyrus Prisse) and _The Instruction of Amenemope_ (New Kingdom/Ramesside, c. 1300-1075 BCE; British Museum Papyrus 10474). Amenemope's ideal is the **"truly silent man"** (_gr mꜣꜥ_), who is calm, self-controlled and trusting in god, as opposed to the **"heated man"** (_šmm_). Proverbs 22:17-24:22 closely parallels Amenemope, which is one of the most famous literary connections in the ancient world.
2. **Funerary texts.** Book of the Dead (_prt m hrw_, "Coming Forth by Day") **chapter 125** holds the "Declaration of Innocence" or "Negative Confession". In it the deceased denies 42 offences before the 42 assessor-gods in the "Hall of the Two Truths" (Maati).
3. **Literary discourses**, above all _**The Eloquent Peasant**_ (Middle Kingdom, c. 1850 BCE). A peasant, Khunanup, is robbed and petitions the high steward Rensi nine times. The text is a sustained meditation on maat as the "breath" of society and on the official as a balance-scale.

This tradition is an extinct religion recorded in its own scripts, hieroglyphic and hieratic. It was not filtered through colonial collectors, but the English renderings are early Egyptology, chiefly Budge, a translator now considered unreliable in details. The light/darkness theme is strong: Ra is the light of maat, Ptahhotep says "Let thy face be bright", and the Peasant says "Truth which is concealed shall be found." That gives good resonance with John 3:19-21.

## Candidate passages

1. **Ptahhotep, maxim 1 (Gunn 1906, p. 42)**

   > Be not proud because thou art learned; but discourse with the ignorant man, as with the sage. For no limit can be set to skill, neither is there any craftsman that possesseth full advantages. Fair speech is more rare than the emerald that is found by slave-maidens on the pebbles.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30508 (text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/30508/pg30508.txt)
   Why: the opening maxim of perhaps the world's oldest surviving wisdom book. Intellectual humility, with a memorable image.

2. **Ptahhotep, maxim 5 (Gunn 1906, pp. 43-44)**

   > If thou be a leader, as one directing the conduct of the multitude, endeavour always to be gracious, that thine own conduct be without defect. Great is Truth, appointing a straight path; never hath it been overthrown since the reign of Osiris.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30508
   Why: "Great is Truth" (Maat) as an unbreakable straight path. It is the core statement of the tradition.

3. **Ptahhotep, maxim 6 (Gunn 1906, p. 44)**

   > Cause not fear among men; for [this] the God punisheth likewise. ... It is another that attaineth by giving unto him that hath not; not he that causeth men dread. ... Live, therefore, in the house of kindliness, and men shall come and give gifts of themselves.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30508
   Why: "Live in the house of kindliness" works as a stand-alone line. Generosity over coercion.

4. **Ptahhotep, maxim 34 (Gunn 1906, p. 55)**

   > Let thy face be bright what time thou livest. That which goeth into the storehouse must come out therefrom; and bread is to be shared. ... It is a man's kindly acts that are remembered of him in the years after his life.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/30508
   Why: brightness of face, sharing, and remembrance after death in one short maxim.

5. **The Eloquent Peasant, third petition (Budge, _Literature of the Ancient Egyptians_, 1914, "The Story of the Educated Peasant Khuenanpu")**

   > Take heed to thyself likewise, for eternity cometh, and behave in such a way that the proverb, 'Righteousness (or truth) is the breath of the nostrils,' may be applicable unto thee.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15932 (text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15932/pg15932.txt)
   Why: "Maat is the breath of the nostrils" is a famous Egyptian saying, quoted here as a proverb inside the text.

6. **The Eloquent Peasant, third petition (Budge 1914)**

   > The equilibrium of the earth is maintained by the doing of what is right. Tell not lies, for thou art a great man. ... Tell not lies, for thou art a pair of scales. ... Thy tongue is the pointer of the scales; thy heart is the weight; thy lips are the two arms of the scales.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15932
   Why: the scales image applied to the judge's own body. "The equilibrium of the earth is maintained by the doing of what is right" stands alone.

7. **The Eloquent Peasant, third petition (Budge 1914)**

   > Truth which is concealed shall be found, and falsehood shall perish. Do not imagine that thou art master of to-morrow, which hath not yet come, for the evils which it may bring with it are unknown.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15932
   Why: **the best John 3:19-21 resonance in the Egyptian corpus.** Hidden truth comes to light.

8. **Book of the Dead ch. 125, Declaration of Innocence, Part 1 (Budge 1914)**

   > Verily, I have come unto thee, I have brought truth unto thee. I have destroyed wickedness for thee. I have not done evil to men. I have not oppressed (or wronged) my family. I have not done wrong instead of right. ... I have not caused pain [to any man]. I have not permitted any man to go hungry. I have made none to weep.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15932
   Why: a selection from the "Negative Confession". "I have made none to weep" is a beautiful single line.

9. **Book of the Dead ch. 125, Part 3 (Budge 1914)**

   > I live upon truth. I feed upon truth. I have performed the commandments of men, and the things which make the gods contented. ... I have given bread to the hungry man, and water to the thirsty man, and apparel to the naked man, and a ferry boat to him that had none.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15932
   Why: works of mercy that closely parallel Matthew 25:35-36 and Isaiah 58:7.

10. **Amenemope, ch. 4 (Budge 1924, _The Teaching of Amen-em-apt_, col. VI, pp. 148-149)**

    > [But] the Ger Maa who sets himself by the side [of the road], He is like a large leafy tree planted in shining (or, fertile) ground (?). It blossoms, it doubles its yield of fruit in the summer. It has its place before the face of its lord (or, Owner). Its foliage (or, fruit) is sweet, the shadow thereof is pleasant...

    Source: https://archive.org/details/teachingofamenem0000eawa (text: https://archive.org/download/teachingofamenem0000eawa/teachingofamenem0000eawa_djvu.txt)
    Why: the "truly silent man" as a flourishing tree, the direct ancestor-image of Psalm 1 and Jeremiah 17:7-8. The preceding lines contrast the "noisy, hot-headed man", a tree withered and burned. For display, consider simplifying Budge's brackets, and say so.

11. **Amenemope, ch. 6 (Budge 1924, pp. 152-153; Budge's sections XXI and XXIV)**

    > Better is one aft of land (about six feet) which the God has given thee Than five thousand ats which thou hast gotten by fraud (or, violence).
    >
    > Better are bread-cakes of flour and water with a loving heart (or, mind) Than rich meats that carry with them bickering and quarrelling.

    Source: https://archive.org/details/teachingofamenem0000eawa
    Why: "better-than" sayings directly paralleled in Proverbs 15:16-17 and 17:1.

12. **Amenemope, ch. 25 (Budge 1924, pp. 174-175, lines 478-483)**

    > Make not a laughing-stock of the blind man. Vex (or, browbeat) not the dwarf, [And] frustrate not the plans of the afflicted (or, lame) man. ... Even if a man mixes the mud [and] the straw It is the God Who is his builder.

    Source: https://archive.org/details/teachingofamenem0000eawa
    Why: dignity of the disabled, and human humility before god as potter or builder.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:19-21.** Peasant (7): "Truth which is concealed shall be found, and falsehood shall perish." The Egyptian judgment scene is also an "exposure" of the heart in the light of maat. The final verdict _mꜣꜥ-ḫrw_ ("true of voice") parallels John's "he that doeth truth".
- **Hebrew Bible: Proverbs 22:17-24:22 (the "Words of the Wise")**, which adapts Amenemope's "thirty chapters" (Prov 22:20). Compare Amenemope ch. 6 "Better are bread-cakes..." with Prov 15:17, and the silent-man tree (10) with Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 17:7-8.
- **Christianity: Matthew 25:35-36** ("I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat..."). Book of the Dead 125 (9) gives a nearly identical list of bread, water, clothing and transport for the needy, about 1,500 years earlier.
- **Confucianism: _Analects_ 2.1 and 12.17** ("To govern means to correct"). Ptahhotep's advice to leaders (2) pairs with the Confucian idea of rule through personal rectitude. Both are father-to-son or teacher-to-student genres.
- **Daoism: _Daodejing_ ch. 8 and 22.** Amenemope's "silent man" versus the "heated man" resembles the Daoist praise of yielding, quietude and water-like humility.
- **Yoruba Ifá: _ìwà_ (character).** Both traditions hold that character, not ritual, is what is finally weighed. See the Ifá narrative in which Ìwà (Character) is the wife of Ọ̀rúnmìlà (ifa.md; listed there as unverified).

## Beware

- **"The 42 Laws of Ma'at" / "42 Ideals of Ma'at" ("I honor virtue", "I am at peace", "I live in truth"...).** These are modern positive rewrites, mostly from the 1990s-2000s Kemetic and New Age milieu, presented as if ancient. The ancient text is a _negative_ declaration of innocence spoken by the dead before 42 named assessor-gods, not a law code. Quote a real translation.
- **"Man, know thyself... and thou shalt know the gods" / "Know thyself" inscribed "in the inner temple at Luxor"** is a common internet claim with no ancient Egyptian source. The Greek maxim was at Delphi, and the Luxor attribution traces to modern esoteric writers.
- **"Kemetic Ten Commandments came from the 42 confessions" / "Moses copied the Negative Confession"** are popular claims. Scholars note thematic overlap, not textual dependence. Amenemope→Proverbs is the solid documented case.
- **Budge's translations** are PD but dated. Some phrases ("Lord of Maati", "Nebertcher") reflect 19th-century readings. Check sense against Lichtheim before display.
- **The "Ptahhotep" dating.** Present it as "attributed to the 5th-Dynasty vizier Ptahhotep (c. 2400 BCE); composed in the Middle Kingdom" rather than as a work written in 2400 BCE.
