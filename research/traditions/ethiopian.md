# Ethiopian philosophy (Zera Yacob, Walda Heywat, Book of the Wise Philosophers)

## Overview

Ethiopia has one of Africa's oldest written intellectual traditions. It was carried in **Gəʿəz** (Ge'ez), the classical liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and nurtured in church and monastic schools, including the _qəne_ poetry schools with their "wax and gold" (_säm-ənna wärq_) double meanings. Two textual strands are central to Ethiopian moral philosophy.

**1. The sapiential, translated tradition.** The _**Mäṣḥafä Fälasfa Ṭäbiban**_ (_Book of the Wise Philosophers_) is a Gəʿəz anthology of sayings attributed to Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Diogenes, Pythagoras and others. It was translated and adapted from an Arabic collection, itself drawing on Greek gnomologia (such as Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq's _Ādāb al-falāsifa_), by **abba Mikaʾel** in about 1510-1522. Related texts include the _Physiologus_ and _The Life and Maxims of Skendes_ (Secundus the Silent). These are wisdom literature of the Mediterranean world, adapted into an Ethiopian Christian idiom.

**2. The Ḥatätas ("Inquiries", from _ḥatäta_, "to investigate").** _**Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob**_ (1667) is the autobiographical philosophical treatise of **Zärʾa Yaʿəqob** (Zera Yacob, "Seed of Jacob", 1599-1692). He was a scholar from Aksum who fled to a cave near the Täkkäze River during the persecutions under Emperor Susənyos, who converted to Roman Catholicism in 1622. Disgusted by sectarian violence between Catholics and Orthodox, he resolves to accept nothing that is not confirmed by the **"light of reason"** or "light of intelligence" (_bərhanä ləbbuna_), which God has placed in every human heart. He argues from creation to a good Creator. He tests religious laws (Christian, Muslim, Jewish) by reason, rejecting those that contradict the order of nature, such as hostility to marriage, extreme fasting and slavery. He holds that **all humans are equal** before God. _**Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät**_, by his disciple **Wäldä Ḥəywät**, is a more practical, sometimes gentler treatise of social ethics, organised as instruction on living well, work, marriage, love of neighbour and education. Its ethic can be summarised in its own words: "Worship God your creator and love all men as yourself."

**The authorship controversy must be disclosed on the site.** The only manuscripts came to Europe in the 1850s via the Capuchin missionary **Giusto d'Urbino**, who worked for the French Orientalist Antoine d'Abbadie. In 1920 **Carlo Conti Rossini** declared the Ḥatätas Giusto's forgery. Neelam Srivastava's chapter in the volume below links Conti Rossini's argument partly to Italian racial theories. **Claude Sumner** (1976), **Getatchew Haile** (who takes the opposite side to Wion in the 2024 volume), **Ralph Lee** and others have defended the texts or the Ethiopian origin of their content. **Anaïs Wion** (2013) and others have revived the forgery case. The 2024 open-access volume _In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob_ (De Gruyter) presents both sides. Display these passages as "from the Ḥatäta attributed to Zera Yacob (1667)", ideally with a footnote on the debate. The light/reason passages are among the most compelling in this whole research set for pairing with John 3:19-21.

**Protocol.** Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity is a living church, but these philosophical texts raise no sacred-restriction issue. They are celebrated in Ethiopia as national philosophical heritage. The sensitivities are the authorship debate and the texts' sharp criticism of both Catholic and Orthodox clergy.

## Candidate passages

All passages were fetched from _In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob_ (2024, CC BY 4.0), PDF: https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/20.500.12657/94328/1/9783110725810.pdf. "HZY" and "HWH" page numbers refer to Lee et al. 2023, as cited in the volume.

1. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob, ch. 5 (trans. Lee et al. 2023, HZY p. 77; quoted in Straughan & O'Connor, ch. 12, _In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob_, p. 231)**

   > If we were to see by this light of our intelligence what is our duty, it cannot deceive us, because our Creator gave us this light that we may be saved by it, and not destroyed [...] all that the light of our intelligence reveals to us is from the fountain of truth

   Why: **the strongest John 3:19-21 resonance in this file.** A God-given light by which we see our duty, given "that we may be saved by it, and not destroyed", echoes John 3:17 ("that the world through him might be saved"). The phrase "light of our intelligence" translates _bərhanä ləbbuna_.

2. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob, ch. 5 (HZY p. 77; Straughan & O'Connor, p. 243)**

   > [T]he creator put the light of intelligence in the human heart, that human beings may perceive good and evil [...]

   Why: a short, display-friendly version of the same thought.

3. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob, ch. 5 (HZY p. 75; Straughan & O'Connor, p. 231)**

   > to the one who searches out, truth will quickly be revealed [...] the one who inquires with the pure reason which the Creator has put into the human heart, to perceive the creation's established order and laws, will find the truth

   Why: a Matthew 7:7 ("seek, and ye shall find") for reason. It is Zera Yacob's methodological creed.

4. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob (HZY p. 83; Straughan & O'Connor, p. 231)**

   > [God has] given intelligence to each and every human being, so that they might recognise truth and lies

   Why: the universality of moral knowledge. The brackets are the volume's.

5. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob, ch. 6 (MS Abbadie 215, fol. 12r; trans. Lee et al. 2023; quoted in the editors' "Introduction" (by Jonathan Egid), _In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob_, p. 45)**

   > All men are equal in the presence of God; and all are intelligent, since they are his creatures; he did not assign one people for life, another for death, one for mercy, another for judgement.

   Why: a 17th-century African statement of universal human equality, against predestination and sectarian exclusion. Very strong.

6. **Ḥatäta Zärʾa Yaʿəqob (HZY p. 88; Straughan & O'Connor, p. 239)**

   > God did not create us as perfect, but rather as understanding beings with the potential for perfection [so that] we may be perfected while we live in this world, and after, be worthy of the reward that our Creator in his wisdom has prepared for us

   Why: a "soul-making" view of life (compare Irenaeus and Keats). It is hopeful on moral growth.

7. **Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät (trans. Sumner 1985, p. 268; quoted in Eyasu Berento Assefa, ch. 5, _In Search of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob_, p. 134)**

   > Mutual love embellishes man's entire life; it makes all our afflictions easier to bear; it adds flavour and sweetness to our whole life; it makes this world the kingdom of heaven

   Alternative translation (Lee et al. 2023, p. 133, given in the volume's note 45): "Mutual love improves all human life. It relieves our suffering, it seasons and sweetens our whole life as it transforms this world into the kingdom of heaven".
   Why: a lovely, short and positive ethic of love. Two translations are available to compare.

8. **Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät (trans. Sumner 1985, p. 267; Eyasu Berento, p. 134)**

   > Do not think that the doctrine of the fools who say the following is good: "The word 'fellow man' is confined only to relatives, or our neighbours, or friends, or members of the same faith". Do not say the same as they do; for all men are our fellow men whether they are good or evil, Christians, [Muslims,] Jews, pagans: all are equal to us and our brothers, because we all are the sons of one father and creator

   Why: an Ethiopian "Good Samaritan" (Luke 10:29, "who is my neighbour?") extended across religions. Very relevant to an interfaith site.

9. **Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät (trans. Sumner 1985, p. 242; Eyasu Berento, p. 136)**

   > the will of God is known by this short statement from our reason that tells us: Worship God your creator and love all men as yourself

   Why: Wäldä Ḥəywät's summary of the moral law, the double commandment (Mark 12:29-31) derived from reason. A golden-rule bridge.

10. **Ḥatäta Wäldä Ḥəywät, ch. 11 "The Teaching of Zärʾa Yaʿəqob" (HWH p. 127; Straughan & O'Connor, p. 239)**

    > The temptations and troubles that happen to human beings in this world are to test them, so that they will be worthy of the reward that their creator will graciously grant them. In fact, no wages are due to anyone who does not work, and no one is worthy of reward who has not been faithful during a period of testing.

    Why: on suffering as testing. It is more conventional, but a good pairing with James 1:2-4 and the Stoics.

11. **Mäṣḥafä Fälasfa Ṭäbiban, preface of the translator abba Mikaʾel (16th c.; trans. Sumner 1998, p. 333; quoted in Eyasu Berento, ch. 5, p. 130)**

    > Behold: these men collected in it [have to say] many good recommendations and counsels and anecdotes as sweet as salt. [...] Their philosophy pleases the heart of man and strengthens his conscience. Philosophers recall in this book the clear and well inspired actions from the wisdom and history of learned persons. It searches for light and is itself the light of the heart. [...]

    Why: "It searches for light and is itself the light of the heart" is a beautiful Ethiopian definition of philosophy. It is the only verified text here from the Book of the Wise Philosophers.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:19-21 and John 1:9** ("the true Light, which lighteth every man"). Zera Yacob's "light of intelligence" in every human heart, given "that we may be saved by it, and not destroyed" (passage 1), is the closest structural parallel in the whole research set. The tension is that for Zera Yacob the light is reason, which can judge even scripture, while for John it is the Logos incarnate.
- **Transcendentalism: Emerson, "Self-Reliance".** Like Zera Yacob (1667), Emerson rejects received authority for the inner light ("A man should learn to detect and watch that gleam of light which flashes across his mind from within"). Both were dissenters from their churches.
- **Early modern European philosophy: Descartes, _Discourse on Method_ (1637) and _Meditations_.** The lumen naturale, methodic doubt, and the argument from the self to God (compare HZY ch. 6 "who created me as intelligent?") are contemporaneous parallels. Much of the scholarship frames Zera Yacob as an "African Descartes", a label the 2024 volume problematises.
- **Islam: the Qur'anic _fiṭra_ (30:30) and Muʿtazilite ethical rationalism.** Moral knowledge is innate and reason can know good and evil. Peter Adamson's chapter in the 2024 volume situates the Ḥatätas near Islamic and Eastern Christian philosophy.
- **Ubuntu / African communitarianism.** Wäldä Ḥəywät's "all men are our fellow men... all are equal to us and our brothers" (passage 8) offers an African universalism that complements relational personhood. Compare Metz's interpretation of ubuntu.
- **Greek wisdom: the gnomic tradition.** The _Book of the Wise Philosophers_ literally carries Socrates and Plato into Gəʿəz. It is a living bridge to Greek philosophical sayings (and, through Arabic, to Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq).

## Beware

- **Stating "Zera Yacob wrote the Hatata in 1667" as plain fact.** Authorship is genuinely contested. Use "attributed to" or note the debate.
- **"Zera Yacob anticipated Descartes / was the father of the Enlightenment"** is a popular framing. It is anachronistic and a target of the 2024 volume's critique.
- **Viral "Zera Yacob quotes"** on quote sites ("To the person who seeks it, truth is immediately revealed", "All men are equal in the presence of God...") are often Sumner's 1976 wording without credit, or loose paraphrases. Always cite the translation.
- **Confusing Zärʾa Yaʿəqob the philosopher (1599-1692) with Emperor Zärʾa Yaʿəqob (r. 1434-1468).** The emperor was a famous religious reformer and writer of the _Mäṣḥafä Bərhan_ ("Book of Light"). They are different people, and the shared name causes frequent errors.
- **The Kebra Nagast** (the "Glory of the Kings", the Solomonic national epic) is sometimes offered as "Ethiopian philosophy". It is a 14th-century royal-religious narrative, not moral philosophy. It is PD in Budge's 1922 translation, but out of scope.
