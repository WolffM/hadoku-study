# Māori whakataukī & Hawaiian ʻōlelo noʻeau

## Overview

The Māori of Aotearoa New Zealand and Kānaka Maoli (Native Hawaiians) are both Polynesian peoples. They descend from the same voyaging ancestors and share a family of languages and core concepts.

**Māori ethics** centres on:

- **whakapapa**: genealogy linking people, land, gods and all living things;
- **mana**: authority, prestige, spiritual power, which is held and enhanced through right conduct;
- **tapu** and **noa**: sacred restriction and its release;
- **tikanga**: correct practice, customary law, "the right way";
- **manaakitanga**: hospitality and care that uplift the mana of others;
- **whanaungatanga**: kinship and relationship;
- **kaitiakitanga**: guardianship of land, water and taonga for future generations;
- **aroha**: love, compassion, empathy.

**Whakataukī** (proverbs, "ancestral sayings") are the condensed teaching form, used in whaikōrero (formal oratory) on the marae and today in education, public service and business. A related term, _whakatauākī_, is sometimes used for a saying whose author is known.

**Hawaiian ethics** shares mana and kapu (tapu) and adds a strong vocabulary of relationship:

- **aloha**: love, compassion, the breath of shared life;
- **lōkahi**: unity and harmony;
- **mālama**: to care for, especially _mālama ʻāina_, care for the land;
- **kuleana**: responsibility and privilege;
- **pono**: righteousness, balance, the right state of things. The state motto, "Ua mau ke ea o ka ʻāina i ka pono", is usually rendered "The life of the land is perpetuated in righteousness";
- **ʻohana**: family.

The **ʻōlelo noʻeau** ("wise sayings") were preserved orally and in 19th-century Hawaiian-language newspapers, and collected above all by **Mary Kawena Pukui** (1895-1986), the great Hawaiian scholar. She began her collection as a teenager and published it as _ʻŌlelo Noʻeau: Hawaiian Proverbs & Poetical Sayings_ (Bishop Museum Press, 1983), with nearly 3,000 entries.

**Transmission and framing.** Both traditions were wholly oral until missionaries introduced writing in the 1820s. Early printed collections of Māori proverbs were made by colonial figures: Governor **Sir George Grey** (_Ko nga Whakatauki_, 1857, Māori text) and the missionary **Rev. Richard Taylor** (_Te Ika a Maui_, 1855, 2nd ed. 1870, Māori with English). Their glosses sometimes misread the sayings or moralise them in a Victorian Christian frame. Modern authoritative collections include Hirini Moko Mead and Neil Grove, _Ngā Pēpeha a ngā Tīpuna_ (2001). For Hawaiian, Pukui was herself Native Hawaiian, writing from inside the tradition but for a bilingual audience. Henry P. Judd's _Hawaiian Proverbs and Riddles_ (1930) is an earlier missionary-descendant collection.

**Cultural protocol: important.** Both are living Indigenous cultures with strong protocols over taonga (treasures, including language and knowledge).

- **Māori:** whakataukī are generally shared freely and used publicly by Māori institutions. Karakia (incantations), whakapapa recitations and tribal-specific pēpeha (tribal identity sayings) are tapu or kin-specific and should not be used as decorative quotes. Use macrons correctly (whakataukī, Māori, Aotearoa). Attribute to a named iwi or speaker where known. The 2011 Waitangi Tribunal report _Ko Aotearoa Tēnei_ (Wai 262) addresses the protection of mātauranga Māori, and is worth reading before publishing.
- **Hawaiian:** misuse of "aloha", "ʻohana" and "mana" in commerce is a sore point. ʻŌlelo noʻeau are publicly taught, but many concern specific places, chiefs or kapu. Prefer those Pukui marks as general sayings. Always include the ʻokina (ʻ) and kahakō (macrons). Sacred chants (mele pule, the Kumulipo) should not be excerpted casually.

## Candidate passages

### Māori: whakataukī (Taylor 1870, PD)

Source for all of nos. 1-6: Richard Taylor, _Te Ika a Maui_ (2nd ed., London: Macintosh, 1870), ch. XVI, pp. 293-298. https://archive.org/details/teikaamauiornew03taylgoog (text: https://archive.org/download/teikaamauiornew03taylgoog/teikaamauiornew03taylgoog_djvu.txt). Māori is given in Taylor's spelling. I corrected the OCR's K→E misreadings ("Ea"→"Ka", "Eo"→"Ko"). Please check the scan.

1. **Taylor no. 12 (p. 294)**

   > Tenei ano a mutu, kei roto i tona ware pungawerewere.
   >
   > The spider is not seen when hid in his web; so the real intention of the man is concealed in the recess of his heart.

   Why: concealment in the heart. It pairs with John 3:20 and with no. 2 below.

2. **Taylor no. 13 (pp. 294-295)**

   > He ta nga kakaho i kitea e te kanohi o te tangata.
   >
   > The slightest movement of the reed waving in the wind is perceived by man's eye, but not that of the heart.

   Why: the hidden heart, which only God, or the light, can see. Compare 1 Samuel 16:7 ("man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart").

3. **Taylor nos. 23-24 (p. 295)**

   > Ko te kai rapu, ko ia te kite. He who seeks finds.
   >
   > Ko ia kahore nei i rapu, te kitea. He who does not seek finds not.

   Why: a near-verbatim parallel to Matthew 7:7-8. Short. Note that Taylor's translation may have been shaped by the Gospel echo.

4. **Taylor no. 30 (p. 296)**

   > Pihi kau ake te wakaaro pai, hauhake tonu iho.
   >
   > The good thought springs up as grass, but it is immediately cut down.

   Why: the fragility of good intentions, close to the Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13).

5. **Taylor no. 42 (p. 297)**

   > Ko Maru kai atu, ko Maru kai mai, ka ngohe ngohe.
   >
   > Give as well as take, and all will be well.

   Why: reciprocity (_utu_ in its positive sense) as the root of social peace. It sits near the golden rule.

6. **Taylor no. 54 (p. 298)**

   > He iti, he iti Kahikatoa.
   >
   > Though small, it is still a Kahikatoa. — A man should not be despised on account of his diminutive size; for he may be like the Kahikatoa (Leptospermum scoparium), which, though only a small tree, is remarkable for its strength and toughness.

   Why: dignity of the small, and humility. Kahikātoa is mānuka.

### Māori: modern attestation

7. **"He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tangata, he tangata, he tangata."** ("What is the most important thing in the world? It is people, it is people, it is people.")
   Attested, in shortened form, as the title of Masters-Awatere, Rarere, Gilbert, Manuel & Scott, "He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tāngata! (What is the most important thing in the world? It is people!)", _Australian Journal of Primary Health_ 25.5 (2019), doi:10.1071/PY19027 (verified via Crossref metadata; licence CC BY-NC-ND 4.0).
   Why: **the best-known whakataukī** and a perfect Ubuntu bridge. It is commonly attributed to **Meri Ngāroto** of Te Aupōuri (see Unverified). The full triple form needs a fetched primary attestation before display.

### Hawaiian: ʻōlelo noʻeau (Pukui 1983, © Bishop Museum; NOT PD)

8. **Pukui, _ʻŌlelo Noʻeau_ #2513, p. 274**

   > ʻO nā hōkū nō nā kiu o ka lani.
   > Translation: The stars are the spies of heaven.
   > Interpretation: The stars look down on everyone and everything.

   Source: Kamehameha Schools, "ʻŌlelo Noʻeau (Set 1)", Mālama Honua Voyage, ʻAukake 2014, https://blogs.ksbe.edu/kaaarona/files/2014/09/MHV_OleloNoeau.pdf
   Why: **a light-from-above image of all deeds being seen**, resonating with John 3:20-21. (The source spells the ʻokina as ‘.)

9. **Pukui #2657, p. 291**

   > Pio ke kukui, pōʻele ka hale. When the light goes out, the house is dark.
   > Said of one whose sight is gone -- he dwells in darkness. Also said when life goes and the darkness of death possesses.

   Source: Muneoka & Keliʻipaʻakaua (comp.), _E Ola ā Kau Kō Kea_ (Hā Kūpuna, University of Hawaiʻi at Mānoa, 2023), no. 96, https://manoa.hawaii.edu/hakupuna/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Olelo_Noeau_PROOF_230724-2.pdf
   Why: light/darkness and death. The kukui (candlenut) was the Hawaiian lamp.

10. **Pukui #1200, p. 130**

    > ʻIke aku, ʻike mai, kōkua aku, kōkua mai; pēlā iho la ka nohona ʻohana. Recognize and be recognized, help and be helped; such is family life.

    Source: _E Ola ā Kau Kō Kea_ (UH Mānoa, 2023), no. 40 (URL as above)
    Why: reciprocity and mutual recognition. A Hawaiian golden rule, and an Ubuntu bridge.

11. **Pukui #229, p. 27**

    > ʻAʻole make ka waʻa i ka ʻale o waho, aia nō i ka ʻale o loko.
    > Translation: A canoe is not swamped by the billows of the ocean, but by the billows near the land.
    > Interpretation: Trouble often comes from one's own people rather than from outsiders.

    Source: Kamehameha Schools 2014 PDF (URL as no. 9)
    Why: communal self-examination. Compare Matthew 7:3 (mote and beam) and Mark 3:25.

12. **Pukui #1229, p. 133**

    > I lohe i ka ʻōlelo a hoʻokō, e ola auaneʻi a laupaʻi. One who hears good counsel and heeds [it] will live to see many descendants.

    Source: _E Ola ā Kau Kō Kea_ (UH Mānoa, 2023), no. 42
    Why: hearing and _doing_. A close parallel to James 1:22 and Matthew 7:24, and to "he that doeth truth".

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:20-21 / Hebrews 4:13** ("all things are naked and opened unto the eyes of him with whom we have to do"). Hawaiian "The stars are the spies of heaven" (8) and Māori "the real intention of the man is concealed in the recess of his heart" (1-2) are the two poles of John 3: concealment in the heart, and the light from above that sees all.
- **Christianity: Matthew 7:7-8** ("seek, and ye shall find") with Māori "Ko te kai rapu, ko ia te kite" (3). This invites reflection on how far the missionary translator heard the Gospel in the proverb.
- **Ubuntu: _umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu_.** "He aha te mea nui o te ao? He tangata" and "ʻIke aku, ʻike mai, kōkua aku, kōkua mai" are among the clearest Pacific counterparts to Southern African relational personhood.
- **Haudenosaunee Seventh Generation / Lakota _mitákuye oyás'iŋ_.** These sit with Māori _kaitiakitanga_ and Hawaiian _mālama ʻāina_ as shared Indigenous ethics of guardianship across generations and species.
- **Ancient Egypt / Ptahhotep and Hebrew Proverbs.** The proverb-as-teaching form is shared. Compare Pukui #1229 (heeding counsel → many descendants) with Proverbs 4:10 ("Hear, O my son, and receive my sayings; and the years of thy life shall be many").
- **Stoicism / Ecclesiastes 1:5** ("The sun also ariseth, and the sun goeth down"). This pairs with Taylor no. 31, "The sun rises to its zenith, and then declines", applied to human life.

## Beware

- **"Hawaiian Ho'oponopono prayer" ("I'm sorry, please forgive me, thank you, I love you")** is a 1980s-2000s New Age adaptation (Morrnah Simeona's "Self I-Dentity through Hoʻoponopono", popularised by Ihaleakala Hew Len and Joe Vitale's _Zero Limits_). It is not the traditional family reconciliation practice described by Pukui, which was a facilitated ʻohana process.
- **"Huna"** (Max Freedom Long's "secret Hawaiian science", from the 1930s onward) is invented. Native Hawaiian scholars reject it.
- **"Ohana means family. Family means nobody gets left behind or forgotten"** is from Disney's _Lilo & Stitch_ (2002), not an ʻōlelo noʻeau.
- **"Aloha" etymologies** ("alo = presence, ha = breath", "the breath of life") are a popular folk etymology, not a documented derivation.
- **"Turn your face to the sun and the shadows fall behind you"** is often labelled a "Māori proverb", but I know of no documented Māori source. Treat it as a modern inspirational saying unless Mead & Grove or Te Ara attest it.
- **Generic "Polynesian proverbs"** on quote sites often mix Māori, Hawaiian and invented material. Always attribute to a specific people and source.

## Unverified (memory only)

- **Meri Ngāroto (Te Aupōuri) as author** of "He aha te mea nui o te ao?", and the full triple "he tangata" form. This is widely stated, including in search-result summaries, but I did not fetch a primary attestation. Te Ara and Mead & Grove are the places to confirm it.
- **"Nā tō rourou, nā taku rourou, ka ora ai te iwi"** ("With your food basket and my food basket the people will thrive"). This is extremely popular in NZ education and public service, but I did not fetch it from a reputable source. Confirm via Te Ara or Mead & Grove.
- **"Ua mau ke ea o ka ʻāina i ka pono"** (Kamehameha III, 1843; Hawaiʻi state motto). This is a well-known fact, but the exact wording and translation were not fetched here.
