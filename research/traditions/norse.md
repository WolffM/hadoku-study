# Norse wisdom: the Hávamál

## Overview

_Hávamál_ ("Sayings of the High One", the High One being Óðinn) is the main body of Old Norse gnomic wisdom. It survives in a single manuscript, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda (GKS 2365 4to, Iceland, c. 1270), where it comes right after _Vǫluspá_. It is not one poem. Scholars treat it as a compilation of several strands. In Bellows's own introductory note (fetched below, using his stanza numbers) these are: the "Hovamol proper" of practical counsel (st. 1–80), the _Loddfáfnismál_ counsels addressed to one Loddfáfnir (st. 111–138), Óðinn's winning of the runes (st. 139–146), the _Ljóðatal_ catalogue of charms (st. 147–165), and two Óðinn episodes about women and the mead of poetry (st. 81–110). The poem was written down in Christian Iceland, but most of its material is generally taken to be older, oral and pre-Christian. Exact dating isn't possible, and claims that it is "Viking-age wisdom" word for word go further than the evidence.

The ethical vision is worldly, social and unsentimental. The good life means keeping your wits (_vit_), being moderate (_hóf_) in drink and speech, being hospitable to the traveller (the opening stanzas on the guest at the door), and above all keeping faithful friendships sealed by exchanged gifts. People are made for each other (_maðr er manns gaman_, "man is man's delight", st. 47). Nobody is wholly good or wholly worthless (st. 133). Death is certain, and what outlives a person is the judgement others pass on their deeds: _orðstírr_, good repute, and _dómr um dauðan hvern_, "the verdict on each dead man" (st. 76–77 in the standard numbering). This is a this-worldly answer to death, with no reward or punishment promised after it.

Some caution applies. Several stanzas are shrewd rather than kind: one may "speak fair, but falsely think" toward an untrusted man (st. 45), and stanzas 84–95 are cynical about women. Modern Heathen religious communities (e.g. Iceland's Ásatrúarfélagið) use the Hávamál devotionally. The poem has also been taken up by white-nationalist movements, and mainstream Heathen organisations explicitly reject that use. Present the passages as poetry and practical wisdom, not as tribal identity.

## Candidate passages

All English is Bellows 1923, fetched from the Wikisource render of
`https://en.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=The_Poetic_Edda_(tr._Bellows)/Hovamol&action=render`
(human page: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Poetic_Edda_(tr._Bellows)/Hovamol). The page markup lost Bellows's line breaks. I restored a line break at each half-stanza boundary, collapsed the caesura spaces, and dropped footnote markers such as "[21]". The words are untouched.

### 1. Hávamál 77 (standard 76): "Cattle die"

> Cattle die, and kinsmen die,
> And so one dies one's self;
> But a noble name will never die,
> If good renown one gets.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. Old Norse (heimskringla.no, st. 76): _Deyr fé, / deyja frændr, / deyr sjalfr it sama, / en orðstírr / deyr aldregi, / hveim er sér góðan getr._
**Source URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/w/index.php?title=The_Poetic_Edda_(tr._Bellows)/Hovamol&action=render ; Old Norse: https://heimskringla.no/wiki/H%C3%A1vam%C3%A1l
**Why:** The best-known lines of Norse wisdom, and a clean, secular answer to death. Pairs naturally with a Death theme.

### 2. Hávamál 78 (standard 77): "the fame of a dead man's deeds"

> Cattle die, and kinsmen die,
> And so one dies one's self;
> One thing I know that never dies,
> The fame of a dead man's deeds.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. Old Norse ends _ek veit einn, / at aldrei deyr: / dómr um dauðan hvern_ ("the judgement on each dead man"). Bellows's "fame" softens the Norse, which speaks of a _verdict_.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Deeds outlast the doer. The Norse "verdict" reading links directly to judgement-of-deeds themes elsewhere (see Bridges).

### 3. Hávamál 47: "man is man's delight"

> Young was I once, and wandered alone,
> And nought of the road I knew;
> Rich did I feel when a comrade I found,
> For man is man's delight.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. Old Norse _maðr er manns gaman_.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Warm and self-contained, and a rare tender moment in the poem.

### 4. Hávamál 50: the lonely fir-tree

> On the hillside drear the fir-tree dies,
> All bootless its needles and bark;
> It is like a man whom no one loves,—
> Why should his life be long?

**Translation:** Bellows 1923.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** A striking image of what isolation does to a person. Works well next to st. 47.

### 5. Hávamál 52: small gifts make friends

> No great thing needs a man to give,
> Oft little will purchase praise;
> With half a loaf and a half-filled cup
> A friend full fast I made.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Modest generosity. Quotable and easy to grasp.

### 6. Hávamál 22: knowing your own faults

> A paltry man and poor of mind
> At all things ever mocks;
> For never he knows, what he ought to know,
> That he is not free from faults.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** The humility theme, from a tradition not usually linked with humility.

### 7. Hávamál 56: a measure of wisdom

> A measure of wisdom each man shall have,
> But never too much let him know;
> Let no man the fate before him see,
> For so is he freest from sorrow.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. Stanzas 54–56 share the same first two lines. St. 55 ends: "For the wise man's heart is seldom happy, / If wisdom too great he has won."
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Moderation applied to knowledge itself. Strong pairing with Ecclesiastes.

### 8. Hávamál 68: the fairest gifts

> Fire for men is the fairest gift,
> And power to see the sun;
> Health as well, if a man may have it,
> And a life not stained with sin.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. "Sin" is Bellows's word. The Old Norse is _án við löst at lifa_, "to live without vice/blemish". Note that "sin" carries a Christian colouring the original lacks.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Fire, sunlight and a blameless life in four lines. Strong for the light theme.

### 9. Hávamál 124: "Nought is so bad as false to be"

> Mingled is love when a man can speak
> To another all his thought;
> Nought is so bad as false to be,
> No friend speaks only fair.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Truthfulness and honest friendship. The friend who tells you hard truths.

### 10. Hávamál 127: name evil openly

> I rede thee, Loddfafnir! and hear thou my rede,—
> Profit thou hast if thou hearest,
> Great thy gain if thou learnest:
> If evil thou knowest, as evil proclaim it,
> And make no friendship with foes.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923 (Loddfáfnismál). For display, the refrain (first three lines) could be cut, leaving the last two lines.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Bringing wrongdoing into the open. The closest Norse counterpart to John 3:20–21.

### 11. Hávamál 133: no one wholly good or wholly bad

> Oft scarcely he knows who sits in the house
> What kind is the man who comes;
> None so good is found that faults he has not,
> Nor so wicked that nought he is worth.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923. (Standard 133 too; Old Norse _né svá illr, at einugi dugi_.)
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Generous and realistic about human nature. Arguably the best single stanza for a cross-traditional page.

### 12. Hávamál 3–4: the guest at the door

> Fire he needs who with frozen knees
> Has come from the cold without;
> Food and clothes must the farer have,
> The man from the mountains come.
>
> Water and towels and welcoming speech
> Should he find who comes to the feast;
> If renown he would get, and again be greeted,
> Wisely and well must he act.

**Translation:** Bellows 1923.
**Source URL:** as above.
**Why:** Hospitality to the stranger as a basic duty. Use st. 3 alone for brevity.

## Beware

- **The "Viking prayer" ("Lo, there do I see my father… Lo, there do I see the line of my people…")** comes from Michael Crichton's novel _Eaters of the Dead_ (1976) and the film _The 13th Warrior_ (1999). It is not in the Hávamál or any Old Norse source. (From my own knowledge; I did not fetch a source for this.)
- **"The Nine Noble Virtues"** is a modern (1970s) Heathen/Odinist list. It is not a Hávamál text, though it draws on it. (From my own knowledge.)
- **Free paraphrases** passed around as "Hávamál" quotes on social media often come from copyrighted modern versions (Auden/Taylor, Crawford, or loose renderings). Check against Bellows, Thorpe or Bray before displaying.
- **Numbering:** a "Hávamál 77" citation can mean different stanzas depending on the edition (see above).

## Bridges

1. **Death and what remains (Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 9:5).** Hávamál 77–78 says reputation never dies. Qoheleth says almost the opposite: the dead have no more reward, "for their memory is forgotten". That makes a sharp tension pairing. Hávamál also matches Epicureanism's this-worldly view of death.
2. **Measure of wisdom (Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 1:18; Greek: Aristotle's mean).** St. 54–56 ("the wise man's heart is seldom happy") lines up almost exactly with "in much wisdom is much grief". The general ethic of _hóf_ (measure) matches Aristotle's doctrine of the mean (_Nicomachean Ethics_ II).
3. **Exposing evil (Christianity: John 3:19–21; Ephesians 5:11).** St. 127, "If evil thou knowest, as evil proclaim it", fits the already-chosen John 3:20–21 well: evildoers hate the light, while deeds done in truth come to it.
4. **Humility and faults (Sufism: Rumi, _Masnavi_ I, "Whoso recognises and confesses his own defects"; Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 7:20).** St. 22 ("not free from faults") and st. 133 ("None so good is found that faults he has not") match Rumi's line (see sufism.md) and "there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and doesn't sin".
5. **Friendship (Greek: Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ VIII–IX; Epicureanism: Vatican Saying 52).** "Man is man's delight" (st. 47) and the gift-exchange friendship stanzas (41–44) can be compared with Aristotle on friendship and with Epicurus on friendship. The Norse version is more transactional ("gifts with gifts requite", st. 42).
6. **Hospitality (Hebrew Bible: Genesis 18; Islam: hadith on honouring the guest).** St. 3–4 parallel Abraham's welcome of the three strangers and the Prophetic hadith "let him honour his guest" (Nawawi 15). See those dossiers for verified text.
