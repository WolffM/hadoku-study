# Mohism (墨家 Mojia)

## Overview

Mohism is the ethics of **兼愛 jian ai**, often translated "universal love". Most specialists now prefer "impartial caring" or "inclusive care", because 兼 means "joining together / taking all inclusively" as opposed to **別 bie**, "discriminating / partial". Mozi (墨子, Master Mo, Mo Di 墨翟, fl. c. 430 BCE) argued that the world's disorder (亂 luan) comes from people caring only for their own: their own selves, families and states. The cure is to "regard the state of others as one's own, the houses of others as one's own, the persons of others as one's self." The goal is **利 li**, benefit: the material welfare, population and order of all under Heaven. For this reason Mohism is often called the world's first consequentialism. Benefit and care are bound together in the formula **兼相愛，交相利** ("impartially care for each other, mutually benefit each other").

Mohist ethics has a religious backbone. **天志 tian zhi**, "the will of Heaven", is the objective standard (法 fa, a measuring-tool like the carpenter's compass and square). Heaven "desires righteousness (義 yi) and abhors unrighteousness" and cares for all people without partiality. The ghosts and spirits (鬼神) reward the good and punish the wicked. From these premises come the Mohists' famous practical doctrines: **非攻 fei gong**, the condemnation of aggressive war; frugality in spending and funerals (節用, 節葬); opposition to lavish court music (非樂); and rejection of fatalism (非命). Mohists also practised what they preached. They were a disciplined organisation of artisans and defensive-warfare specialists who travelled to besieged cities to help defend them, and the _Mozi_ contains manuals on fortification.

Historically, Mohism was Confucianism's chief rival in the Warring States period. Mencius complained that the doctrines of Mozi and Yang Zhu "filled the world". The movement vanished as an organised school after the Qin-Han unification, and the text survived only marginally until Qing scholars, notably Sun Yirang (孫詒讓, 1894), revived its study. The core ethical chapters (8-37) come in triads (上/中/下, I/II/III), probably records of three Mohist sub-schools. Later chapters (40-45, the "Mohist Canons") contain sophisticated logic, optics and mechanics. It is not a living religious community today, but it is widely read in China and in comparative ethics.

## Candidate passages

### 1. Ch. 14, "Universal Love (I)" 兼愛上: where disorder comes from

- **Translation:** Mei (1929), archive.org (URL above)
- **Text:** "Suppose we try to locate the cause of disorder, we shall find it lies in the want of mutual love."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/兼愛上): 當察亂何自起？起不相愛。
- **Why:** The whole Mohist diagnosis in one sentence. Short and very displayable.

### 2. Ch. 14, "Universal Love (I)": if all loved universally

- **Translation:** Mei (1929). OCR: "uiiivervsally" → [universally]
- **Text:** "If every one in the world will love universally; states not attacking one another; houses not disturbing one another; thieves and robbers becoming extinct; emperor and ministers, fathers and sons, all being affectionate and filial — if all this comes to pass the world will be orderly."
- **Chinese:** 若使天下兼相愛，國與國不相攻，家與家不相亂，盜賊無有，君臣父子皆能孝慈，若此則天下治。
- **Why:** A positive vision of peace grounded in love. It sits naturally beside Isaiah 2:4 and the Beatitudes.

### 3. Ch. 15, "Universal Love (II)" 兼愛中: others as oneself

- **Translation:** Mei (1929)
- **Text:** "But what is the way of universal love and mutual aid? Motse said: It is to regard the state of others as one's own, the houses of others as one's own, the persons of others as one's self."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/兼愛中): 視人之國若視其國，視人之家若視其家，視人之身若視其身。
- **Why:** This is the Mohist "love your neighbour **as yourself**" (Leviticus 19:18; Mark 12:31), stated about four centuries before the Gospels. It is the single best candidate from this tradition.

### 4. Ch. 15, "Universal Love (II)": the social fruits of mutual love

- **Translation:** Mei (1929). OCR: "on^-afibther" → [one another]
- **Text:** "When feudal lords love one another there will be no more war; when heads of houses love one another there will be no more mutual usurpation; when individuals love one another there will be no more mutual injury. … When all the people in the world love one another, then the strong will not overpower the weak, the many will not oppress the few, the wealthy will not mock the poor, the honoured will not disdain the humble, and the cunning will not deceive the simple."
- **Chinese:** 諸侯相愛，則不野戰；家主相愛，則不相篡；人與人相愛，則不相賊；…天下之人皆相愛，强不執弱，衆不劫寡，富不侮貧，貴不敖賤，詐不欺愚。
- **Why:** The second half is a strong standalone protection of the weak, the few, the poor, the humble and the simple. It echoes Hebrew prophetic ethics.

### 5. Ch. 15, "Universal Love (II)": reciprocity of love

- **Translation:** Mei (1929)
- **Text:** "Whoever loves others is loved by others; whoever benefits others is benefited by others; whoever hates others is hated by others; whoever injures others is injured by others."
- **Chinese:** 夫愛人者，人必從而愛之；利人者，人必從而利之；惡人者，人必從而惡之；害人者，人必從而害之。
- **Why:** Crisp and parallel. Compare Luke 6:38 ("with the same measure that ye mete") and the Buddhist law of kamma in Dhammapada 1-2.

### 6. Ch. 16, "Universal Love (III)" 兼愛下: replace partiality

- **Translation:** Mei (1929)
- **Text:** "Motse said: Partiality is to be replaced by universality. But how is it that partiality can be replaced by universality? Now, when every one regards the states of others as he regards his own, who would attack the others' states? Others are regarded like self."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/兼愛下): 兼以易別。
- **Why:** 兼以易別 ("replace partiality with impartiality") is Mozi's four-character slogan. "Others are regarded like self" is a good short pull-quote.

### 7. Ch. 17, "Condemnation of Offensive War (I)" 非攻上: the stolen peaches

- **Translation:** Mei (1929)
- **Text:** "Suppose a man enters the orchard of another and steals the other's peaches and plums. Hearing of it the public will condemn it; laying hold of him the authorities will punish him. Why? Because he injures others to profit himself."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/非攻上): 今有一人，入人園圃，竊其桃李，衆聞則非之，上爲政者得則罰之。
- **Why:** This opens one of antiquity's great anti-war arguments, which builds from petty theft up to conquest. It pairs with the next passage.

### 8. Ch. 17, "Condemnation of Offensive War (I)": one murder, many murders

- **Translation:** Mei (1929)
- **Text:** "The murder of one person is called unrighteous and incurs one death penalty. Following this argument, the murder of ten persons will be ten times as unrighteous and there should be ten death penalties; the murder of a hundred persons will be a hundred times as unrighteous and there should be a hundred death penalties. All the gentlemen of the world know that they should condemn these things, calling them unrighteous. But when it comes to the great unrighteousness of attacking states, they do not know that they should condemn it. On the contrary, they applaud it, calling it righteous."
- **Chinese:** 殺一人謂之不義，必有一死罪矣。…
- **Why:** A non-violence passage of startling modernity. It parallels the line "One murder makes a villain, millions a hero" (Beilby Porteus, 1759) and Augustine's pirate and Alexander (_City of God_ 4.4). It could be trimmed to the first and last two sentences.

### 9. Ch. 3, "On Dyeing" 所染: what we are dyed in

- **Translation:** Mei (1929). OCR: "Watcliing" → [Watching], "sigked" → [sighed], "Wiiat" → [What], "wbat" → [what], "difierent" → [different]
- **Text:** "Watching a dyer of silk at work, Motse sighed, saying: What is dyed in blue becomes blue, what is dyed in yellow becomes yellow. When the silk is put in a different dye, its colour becomes also different. Having been dipped in five times, it has changed its colour five times. Therefore dyeing should be done with great care."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/所染): 染於蒼則蒼，染於黃則黃，所入者變，其色亦變。
- **Why:** A gentle, image-driven passage on moral influence and the company we keep. Compare 1 Corinthians 15:33 ("evil communications corrupt good manners") and Xunzi's indigo image (see the confucianism file).

### 10. Ch. 47, "Esteem for Righteousness" 貴義: nothing more valuable

- **Translation:** Mei (1929). OCR: "tte" → [the]
- **Text:** "Motse said: Of the multitude of things none is more valuable than righteousness. Suppose we say to a person: We shall give you a hat and shoes on condition you let us cut off your hands and feet. Would he agree to this? Of course, he will not agree. Why? Just because hats and shoes are not so valuable as hands and feet. Again (if we say), we shall give you the whole world on condition you let us kill you. Would he agree to this? Of course he will not agree. Why? Just because the world is not so valuable as one's person. Yet people have struggled against one another for a single principle. This shows righteousness is even more valuable than one's person."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/貴義): 萬事莫貴於義。
- **Why:** This strikingly echoes Mark 8:36 ("what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul?"), with a twist: righteousness is worth more even than one's life.

### 11. Ch. 46, "Keng Chu" 耕柱: water or fuel?

- **Translation:** Mei (1929). OCR: "reiEforee" → [reinforce]. Context: Wu Matse (巫馬子) has just said, "Though you love universally the world cannot be said to be benefited; though I do not love (universally) the world cannot be said to be injured."
- **Text:** "Motse answered: Suppose a conflagration is on. One person is fetching water to extinguish it, and another is holding some fuel to reinforce it. Neither of them has yet accomplished anything, but which one do you value? Wu Matse answered that he approved of the intention of the person who fetches water and disapproved of the intention of the person who holds fuel. Motse said: (In the same manner,) do I approve of my intention and disapprove of yours."
- **Chinese** (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/耕柱): 子墨子曰：「今有燎者於此，一人奉水將灌之，一人摻火將益之，功皆未至，子何貴於二人？」
- **Why:** A memorable parable about intention and taking sides, and a good answer to "what difference does my small effort make?"

## Bridges

1. **Love of neighbour as self: Judaism/Christianity (Lev 19:18; Mark 12:31).** "The persons of others as one's self" (視人之身若視其身, passage 3) is the closest classical-Chinese match to the second great commandment. The Mohist ground is Heaven's own impartial care: "How do we know Heaven loves the people? Because it teaches them all" (ch. 26, Mei, verified). The Chinese is 以其兼而明之 (https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/墨子/天志上), and 明 literally means "_enlightens / illumines_ them all", a light-image Mei's "teaches" hides. This mirrors Matthew 5:45, where the Father "maketh his sun to rise on the evil and on the good."
2. **Tension with Confucianism: graded love.** Mencius (3B9) charged that Mozi's impartial love "does not acknowledge the peculiar affection due to a father" (paraphrase; check Legge's wording). In the Mozi itself, Wu Matse makes the Confucian case: "I love the people of Tsou better than the people of Ch'u … my parents better than the other members of my family, and myself better than my parents. This, because of their nearness to me" (ch. 46, Mei, verified). The same debate runs through Christian ethics: the _ordo amoris_ of Augustine and Aquinas vs. impartial agape.
3. **Non-violence: Buddhism, Jainism, Christian pacifism.** The "one murder / a hundred murders" argument (passage 8) anticipates the Dhammapada's "All men tremble at punishment" (129) and Erasmus's _Complaint of Peace_ (1517). Unlike Jains and Buddhists, Mohists were not absolute pacifists. They practised _defensive_ warfare and helped besieged cities, which makes a useful nuance.
4. **Intention vs. outcome: Kant and the Gita.** The water-or-fuel parable (passage 11) values the _will_ before results, which is surprising from a "consequentialist". Compare Bhagavad Gita 2.47 on acting without attachment to the fruits.
5. **The company we keep: Proverbs 13:20 / 1 Cor 15:33.** "On Dyeing" (passage 9) expresses the same moral psychology as "He that walketh with wise men shall be wise."

## Beware

- **"Universal love"** is Mei's (and Legge's era's) rendering of 兼愛. It risks implying an emotional, agapeic love. Most scholars now prefer "impartial caring", because 愛 here includes concern-for-welfare and is tied to 利 (benefit). If a passage is displayed, a one-line gloss would help.
- **Not the Mohist Canon.** The later logical chapters (墨經, chs. 40-45) are by later Mohists, not Mozi, so do not attribute their dialectics to him.
- **Triad chapters.** Each core doctrine exists in three versions (上/中/下). Cite the specific chapter, as above, because wording differs a lot between versions.
- Few popular quotes are falsely attributed to Mozi; the risk here is the reverse. Lines like "love your neighbour as yourself" sound so Christian that readers may suspect a fabrication, so the chapter citation and Chinese text should be shown.
- **"Motse" / "Mo Tzu" / "Mo-tse" / "Micius"** are all the same person, Mozi. "Micius" is the Latinised form used for China's 2016 quantum satellite.
