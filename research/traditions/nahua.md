# Nahua / Aztec wisdom (huehuetlahtolli & Nezahualcoyotl)

## Overview

The Nahua peoples of central Mexico, including the Mexica of Tenochtitlan (the "Aztecs"), the Acolhua of Texcoco and the Tlaxcalans, built a highly literate oral-performative culture. Its ethical heart lies in two genres:

- **huēhuetlahtōlli**, "speech of the elders" or "ancient word": formal, polished exhortations delivered by parents to children, rulers to subjects, and midwives to newborns;
- **cuīcatl**, song-poetry, especially the philosophical "flower and song" (_in xōchitl in cuīcatl_) poems associated with the **tlamatinimê** ("those who know things", sages).

The ethics they express is one of **moderation and the middle way**, often summarised by later scholars as "the earth is slippery". That image comes from Book 6; see Unverified. Life is precarious and fleeting, so a person must cultivate a "face and heart" (_in īxtli in yōllōtl_, the Nahua idiom for person or character). They do so through humility, self-restraint, diligence, reverence and respectful speech. The poems press the existential question of whether anything on earth is real or lasting ("Do we truly live on earth?", a refrain as rendered by León-Portilla; the exact Nahuatl was not verified here), and answer with transience, friendship and song offered to the **Ipalnemohuani**, "Giver of Life".

**Nezahualcoyotl** ("Fasting Coyote", 1402-1472) was the tlatoani (king) of Texcoco, a celebrated lawgiver, engineer and poet. Many poems are attributed to him, but the attributions are often insecure. The songs survive in two late-16th-century manuscripts: the _Cantares Mexicanos_ (Biblioteca Nacional de México) and the _Romances de los señores de la Nueva España_. They were written down in alphabetic Nahuatl after the Conquest, in a Christianising missionary environment. Some, including texts mentioning "Dios", show Christian adaptation. His descendant Fernando de Alva Ixtlilxóchitl (c. 1578-1650) promoted his image as a quasi-monotheist philosopher-king. Treat attribution claims cautiously.

**Transmission and framing.** The huehuetlahtolli were memorised and taught in the calmecac and telpochcalli schools. They reach us mainly through the Franciscan friar **Bernardino de Sahagún**, who with Nahua scholars (trilingual graduates of the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco such as Antonio Valeriano) and elder informants compiled the _Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España_. Its bilingual Nahuatl/Spanish manuscript is the **Florentine Codex** (completed c. 1577). **Book 6, "Rhetoric and Moral Philosophy"**, holds about 40 huehuetlahtolli plus proverbs, riddles and metaphors. Sahagún's purpose was missionary: to know the "idolatries" in order to uproot them, and to show the Nahuas' moral excellence. His informants were elite men speaking in a colonial setting a generation after the Conquest. Andrés de Olmos's earlier collection (Juan Bautista's _Huehuetlahtolli_, 1600) was openly Christianised. The English passages below are a further remove: Bancroft (1875) paraphrased Sahagún's _Spanish_, not the Nahuatl.

There is no protocol restriction on displaying these texts. Nahua communities and Nahuatl language revitalisation are living, but the huehuetlahtolli and cantares are treated as public literary heritage in Mexico. Nezahualcoyotl's portrait is on the 100-peso note, with lines of his verse.

## Candidate passages

1. **Huehuetlahtolli, "A parent's discourse to his sons" (Sahagún, _Historia general_ Bk 6; English paraphrase in Bancroft, _Native Races_ vol. 2, 1875, ch. on education)**

   > Look to it that you are not puffed up with pride, that you are neither obstinate, nor of a weak, vacillating mind, but take heed to be meek and humble and to put your trust in the gods, lest they visit your transgressions upon you, for from them nothing can be hidden, they punish how and whom they please.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42808 (text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/42808/pg42808.txt)
   Why: humility, plus "from them nothing can be hidden". That last phrase echoes John 3:20 ("lest his deeds should be reproved").

2. **Same discourse (Bancroft 1875)**

   > Secondly, my sons, endeavor to live at peace with your fellow-men. Treat all with deference and respect; if any speak ill of you answer them not again; be kind and affable to all, yet converse not too freely with any; slander no man; be patient, returning good for evil, and the gods will amply avenge your wrongs.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42808
   Why: "returning good for evil". This makes a striking parallel to Romans 12:17-21 and Matthew 5. Note that the wording passes through a Franciscan, which deserves a caveat on display.

3. **Same discourse (Bancroft 1875)**

   > Lastly, my children, be not wasteful of your goods nor of your time, for both are precious; at all seasons pray to the gods and take counsel with them; be diligent about those things which are useful.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42808
   Why: short and practical. Time as something precious.

4. **Same discourse (Bancroft 1875)**

   > I am not what I am by my own asking; never did I say, I wish to be so and so, I desire this or that honor; the gods have done me this honor of their own will, for surely all is theirs, and all that is given comes from their hand.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42808
   Why: a ruler's humility. Office is a gift, not an achievement.

5. **Huehuetlahtolli, address to a newly elected ruler (Sahagún Bk 6; Bancroft 1875, "Aztec oratory")**

   > He who has lately left us was strong and valorous: for a few short days he was lent to us, then like a vision he slipped from our midst, and his passing was as a dream, for the Lord our God hath called him to rest with the dead kings, his ancestors...

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/42808
   Why: the Nahua idea of life as "lent" and like a dream. It is on death.

6. **Cantares Mexicanos, "Songs of the Prince Nezahualcoyotl" (_Ycuic Nezahualcoyotzin_), v. 3 (Brinton, _Ancient Nahuatl Poetry_, 1887, no. XXIII)**

   > For this, for this, I weep, I Nezahualcoyotl, inasmuch as I am to go, I am to be lost in death, I must leave thee; my God, the Giver of Life, thou commandest me, that I go forth, that I be lost, alas.

   Nahuatl (Brinton): _Quiyon quiyon caya nichocaya ya ni Nezahualcoyotl huiya queni yeno yaz o ya nipolihuiz oya miquitla ye nimitzcahua noteouh ypalnemo o tinechnahuatia ye niaz nipolihuiz aya, yo._

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219 (text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/12219/pg12219.txt)
   Why: the poet-king facing death before the Giver of Life. Bilingual text is available.

7. **Cantares Mexicanos, "Another plain song of the Mexicans", v. 2 (Brinton 1887, no. V; voiced by "Yoyontzin", a name of Nezahualcoyotl)**

   > True it is that my possessions shall perish, my friendships, their home and their house; thus I, O Yoyontzin, pour forth songs to the Giver of Life.

   Nahuatl (Brinton): _In mach noca ompolihuiz in cohuayotl mach noca in icniuhyotl in ononoya in ye ichan; ye nio Yoyontzin on cuicatillano ye ipalnemohuani._

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219
   Why: transience answered with song and praise. It is short.

8. **Cantares Mexicanos, no. XI "Another", v. 8 (Brinton 1887)**

   > But what can I speak truly here of the Giver of Life? We only dream, we are plunged in sleep; I speak here on earth; but never can we speak in worthy terms here.

   Nahuatl (Brinton): _Azo tle nello nicyaitohua nican ipalnemohua, zan tontemiqui y, zan toncochitlehuaco, nicitoa in tlalticpac ye ayac huel tontiquilhuia ye nicana._

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219
   Why: the classic Nahua theme of life as dream and the limits of speech about the divine. It is apophatic.

9. **Cantares Mexicanos, no. II "A spring song, an Otomi song, a plain song", v. 5 (Brinton 1887)**

   > How much, alas, shall I weep on earth? Truly I have lived here in vain illusion; I say that whatever is here on earth must end with our lives.

   Nahuatl (Brinton): _Quenin ah nichocaz in tlalticpac? ye nican onca nemoayá ninoztlacahuia, nicitoa aço zan ye ixquich in nican in tlalticpac ontlamian toyolia..._

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219
   Why: a memento mori, close to Ecclesiastes.

10. **Cantares Mexicanos, no. I "Song at the Beginning" (_Cuicapeuhcayotl_), v. 8 (Brinton 1887)**

    > And I said, truly there is no good spot here on earth, truly in some other bourne there is gladness; For what good is this earth? Truly there is another life in the hereafter.

    Nahuatl (Brinton): _Auh nic itoaya tlacazo amo qualcan in tlalticpac ye nican, tlacazo occecni in huilohuayan, in oncan ca in netlamachtilli; tlezannen in tlalticpac? tlacazo occecni yoliliz ximoayan..._

    Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219
    Why: the hope of a "place beyond". Note possible Christian influence on the post-Conquest text.

11. **Cantares Mexicanos, no. XI, v. 1 (Brinton 1887)**

    > I lift my voice in wailing, I am afflicted, as I remember that we must leave the beautiful flowers, the noble songs; let us enjoy ourselves for a while, let us sing, for we must depart forever, we are to be destroyed in our dwelling place.

    Nahuatl (Brinton): _Nicchocaehua, nicnotlamati, nicelnamiqui ticauhtehuazque yectliya xochitl yectli yancuicatl; ma octonahuiacan, ma oc toncuicacan cen tiyahui tipolihui ye ichan, etc._

    Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12219
    Why: the "flower and song" carpe diem, paired with mourning.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:20** ("every one that doeth evil hateth the light... lest his deeds should be reproved"). The father's warning that "from them nothing can be hidden" (passage 1) and the Book 6 adage about the wrongdoer who "thinks nobody knows, and everybody knows" (see Unverified) both speak to deeds exposed.
- **Christianity: Romans 12:17-21 / Matthew 5:44.** "Be patient, returning good for evil" (passage 2). Flag that Sahagún's Spanish may have shaded it toward Christian phrasing.
- **Hebrew Bible: Ecclesiastes 1:2-4 and 9:7-10** ("Go thy way, eat thy bread with joy"). Passages 9 and 11 make a near-exact thematic match: vanity, transience, and enjoy-while-you-may.
- **Ancient Egypt: the "Harper's Songs"** ("Follow thy desire while thou livest") and **Ptahhotep**. The Egyptian instruction-genre of father to son is the closest formal relative of the huehuetlahtolli. Compare Ptahhotep §1 on humility with passage 1.
- **Buddhism: impermanence (_anicca_)** and the Diamond Sutra's closing verse (life "like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow"), set beside "We only dream, we are plunged in sleep" (passage 8).
- **Confucianism: the Analects on filial instruction and ritual propriety.** Nahua emphasis on proper speech, decorum and the "middle way" parallels _li_ and the Doctrine of the Mean (_Zhongyong_).

## Beware

- **"Not forever on earth, only a little while here..." / "I love the song of the mockingbird..." / "Like a painting we will be erased"** and other "Nezahualcoyotl poems" circulate widely in English and Spanish. Most come from León-Portilla's or Garibay's 20th-century renderings (copyrighted), and some are loose paraphrases or invented. Several have no secure attribution to Nezahualcoyotl even in the manuscripts. Always cite the manuscript and the translator.
- **"In Lak'ech Ala K'in" ("You are my other me")** is often presented as an ancient Maya or "Aztec" greeting-philosophy. It comes from Luis Valdez's 1971 poem "Pensamiento Serpentino" and Domingo Martínez Paredez's 1960s writing, and is not attested in classical sources. It is Maya-styled in any case, not Nahua.
- **"Toltec wisdom" / _The Four Agreements_** (Don Miguel Ruiz, 1997) is a modern New Age work with no basis in Nahua sources.
- **Bancroft and Brinton** are PD but heavily mediated: a Franciscan Spanish version of Nahuatl speech (Bancroft), or an outdated Victorian translation (Brinton). Label them "as rendered by ...". Also note that Sahagún's Book 6 Spanish is often a summary, not a translation, of the Nahuatl.

## Unverified (memory only)

- **"The earth is slippery, slick"** (Florentine Codex Bk 6, the father's exhortation to his daughter, with the image of walking a narrow ridge between two abysses). This is the most famous huehuetlahtolli image and the source of Burkhart's book title. I could not fetch Anderson & Dibble's wording (in copyright, not online), so I did not quote it.
- **Book 6 ch. 41 adage on the hidden wrongdoer.** In the 1829 Bustamante Spanish text (archive.org bub_gb_MmMAooljV18C, adages chapter), the gloss reads "Esto se dice de aquel que hizo algún mal, piensa que nadie lo sabe, y sábenlo todos" ("This is said of one who did some evil and thinks nobody knows it, and everyone knows it"). The OCR is badly garbled and the proverb itself (the line above the gloss) is unreadable, so this needs checking against a clean edition. It would make a superb John 3:20 pairing if confirmed.
