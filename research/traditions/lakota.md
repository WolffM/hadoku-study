# Lakota (and Dakota) ethics

## Overview

The Lakota (Teton) are the westernmost division of the **Očhéthi Šakówiŋ**, the "Seven Council Fires", often called "Sioux", an exonym. The other divisions are the Dakota (Santee) and the Yankton-Yanktonai (Ihanktonwan / Ihanktonwanna). The seven Lakota bands include the Oglála, Sičháŋǧu (Brulé), Hunkpapa and Mnikȟówožu. Lakota ethics is a kinship ethic. The ideal of **wólakȟota** (peace, being Lakota) is achieved through right relationship within the **thiyóšpaye** (extended family) and, by extension, with all beings. This is summed up in the phrase **mitákuye oyás'iŋ**, "all my relatives" or "we are all related". It is said at the beginning and end of prayers and ceremonies, and Osage theologian George Tinker compares its function to "Amen". Its scope includes animals, plants, stones and waters, not only humans. The sacred is **Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka**, "Great Mystery" or "Great Holy", and in prayer **Tȟuŋkášila**, "Grandfather".

The **Lakota virtues** are taught today in slightly varying lists of four, six or seven. The Aktá Lakota Museum's list of seven, fetched below, is:

- Wóčekiya (prayer)
- Waóhola (respect)
- Wótakuye (kinship)
- Wóksape (wisdom)
- Wówauŋšila (compassion)
- Wačáŋtognaka (generosity)
- Wowačíŋtaŋka (fortitude)

These are spelled as the page spells them. Other orthographies, such as the Lakota Language Consortium standard, spell them differently.

Oglala Lakota College names "respect, generosity, wisdom, fortitude, bravery and humility". The classical four of the 19th-century camp circle were bravery, generosity, fortitude and wisdom. Generosity is central. The **giveaway** (_wópila_ / _othúhaŋ_) redistributes wealth at every major life event. The sources fetched below also stress truthfulness, silence and self-control.

**Transmission and framing.** Lakota knowledge was and is oral. It is carried in the language, in winter counts (pictographic calendars), in story cycles such as the Iktómi stories, in ceremony, and in kin teaching. The written record is heavily mediated:

- missionary and government ethnographers, such as James R. Walker's Oglala interviews (1896-1914) and Frances Densmore;
- Lakota/Dakota collaborators, such as George Sword and Ella Deloria;
- most famously John G. Neihardt's _Black Elk Speaks_ (1932). Raymond DeMallie's publication of the interview transcripts, _The Sixth Grandfather_ (1984), showed how much of the book's poetic framing, including the famous lament "a people's dream died there", is Neihardt's.

The two most useful **public-domain, Native-authored** books are _The Soul of the Indian_ (1911) by **Charles A. Eastman (Ohíye S'a)** and _My People the Sioux_ (1928) by **Luther Standing Bear (Mató Nážiŋ)**. Eastman was **Santee Dakota**, not Lakota, so attribute his passages to "Dakota" or "Ohiyesa (Santee Dakota)". He also wrote deliberately for a white Christian and Progressive-era audience, idealising a pre-reservation past. Standing Bear was **Oglala Lakota** and a Carlisle student, and his books were edited by E. A. Brininstool.

**Cultural protocol.** In 1993 Lakota, Dakota and Nakota spiritual leaders issued a "Declaration of War Against Exploiters of Lakota Spirituality" in response to commercialised and "New Age" use of ceremonies. Do not display ceremonial texts or prayers, such as the pipe, the inípi (sweat lodge), the Sun Dance, the vision quest or the White Buffalo Calf Woman narrative, as decorative wisdom. Using _mitákuye oyás'iŋ_ as a stand-alone display phrase is common, but it is a prayer phrase, so present it with context. The safest material is the virtues as publicly taught by Lakota educational institutions, and the published autobiographical teaching of Eastman and Standing Bear.

## Candidate passages

1. **Charles Eastman (Ohiyesa, Dakota), _The Soul of the Indian_ (1911), ch. II "The Family Altar"**

   > In the life of the Indian there was only one inevitable duty,--the duty of prayer--the daily recognition of the Unseen and Eternal. ... After the bath, he stands erect before the advancing dawn, facing the sun as it dances upon the horizon, and offers his unspoken orison. ... Each soul must meet the morning sun, the new, sweet earth, and the Great Silence alone!

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340 (text: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/340/pg340.txt)
   Why: a light image. Each person stands alone before the dawn. The last sentence works by itself.

2. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. I "The Great Mystery"**

   > The worship of the "Great Mystery" was silent, solitary, free from all self-seeking. It was silent, because all speech is of necessity feeble and imperfect; therefore the souls of my ancestors ascended to God in wordless adoration. It was solitary, because they believed that He is nearer to us in solitude, and there were no priests authorized to come between a man and his Maker.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: a classic statement of apophatic, non-institutional devotion.

3. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. IV "Barbarism and the Moral Code"**

   > If you ask him: "What is silence?" he will answer: "It is the Great Mystery!" "The holy silence is His voice!" If you ask: "What are the fruits of silence?" he will say: "They are self-control, true courage or endurance, patience, dignity, and reverence. Silence is the cornerstone of character."

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: very quotable. "Silence is the cornerstone of character" stands on its own.

4. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. IV**

   > Long before I ever heard of Christ, or saw a white man, I had learned from an untutored woman the essence of morality. With the help of dear Nature herself, she taught me things simple but of mighty import. I knew God. I perceived what goodness is. I saw and loved what is really beautiful. Civilization has not taught me anything better!

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: moral knowledge as given before and outside the Church. It pairs provocatively with John 3 and with Romans 2:14-15 (the law "written in their hearts").

5. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. II**

   > Silence, love, reverence,--this is the trinity of first lessons; and to these she later adds generosity, courage, and chastity.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: a compact catalogue of virtues, taught by the mother.

6. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. IV**

   > It was our belief that the love of possessions is a weakness to be overcome. Its appeal is to the material part, and if allowed its way it will in time disturb the spiritual balance of the man. Therefore the child must early learn the beauty of generosity. He is taught to give what he prizes most, and that he may taste the happiness of giving, he is made at an early age the family almoner.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: the ethic of the giveaway in the tradition's own voice.

7. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. I**

   > It is my personal belief, after thirty-five years' experience of it, that there is no such thing as "Christian civilization." I believe that Christianity and modern civilization are opposed and irreconcilable, and that the spirit of Christianity and of our ancient religion is essentially the same.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: a bold bridge text by a Christian Dakota physician. It could be paired directly with John 3.

8. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. VI "On the Border-Land of Spirits"**

   > The attitude of the Indian toward death, the test and background of life, is entirely consistent with his character and philosophy. Death has no terrors for him; he meets it with simplicity and perfect calm, seeking only an honorable end as his last gift to his family and descendants.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: on death. Note that the sentence continues with courting death in battle, so trim carefully.

9. **Eastman, _The Soul of the Indian_, ch. V "The Unwritten Scriptures"**

   > Naturally magnanimous and open-minded, the red man prefers to believe that the Spirit of God is not breathed into man alone, but that the whole created universe is a sharer in the immortal perfection of its Maker.

   Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/340
   Why: the theological basis of _mitákuye oyás'iŋ_, in English.

10. **Luther Standing Bear (Oglala Lakota), _My People the Sioux_ (1928), ch. "My First Buffalo", p. 65**

    > It was, 'Son, always remember that a man who tells lies is never liked by anybody.' So, instead of trying to cheat, I told the truth; and it made me feel happier.

    Source: https://archive.org/details/mypeoplesioux00stan_0 (text: https://archive.org/download/mypeoplesioux00stan_0/mypeoplesioux00stan_0_djvu.txt)
    Why: a Lakota father's teaching on truthfulness, told by a Lakota author, PD and short. It sits very close to "he that doeth truth".

11. **Aktá Lakota Museum & Cultural Center, "Seven Lakota Values": Generosity, Wačáŋtognaka (web page, © St. Joseph's Indian School)**

    > Giveaways have always been part of Lakota society. At important events, the family gathers their belongings and sets them out for any person in the community to take. "What you give away, you keep; what you keep, you lose" is an old Lakota saying.

    Source: https://aktalakota.stjo.org/lakota-culture/seven-lakota-values/
    Why: a genuine-sounding proverb with an institutional Lakota-serving source. Provenance is moderate: "an old Lakota saying" is not traced further. Not PD. Use the proverb with credit.

12. **Oglala Lakota College, "Lakota Perspective" (from the 2003 Oglala Lakota Self Study Report; © OLC)**

    > The Lakota perspective is a world view which derives from the oral creation story of the Lakota people and is, therefore, a living, dynamic view handed down from generation to generation. At the base of this view is an unchanging adherence to the humanistic values of respect, generosity, wisdom, fortitude, bravery and humility.

    Source: https://www.olc.edu/about/vision-mission-statement/lakota-perspective/
    Why: the tribal college's own definition of the virtues. Ideal as framing text, with permission if displayed.

## Bridges

- **Christianity: John 3:19-21.** Eastman (passage 7) argues that "the spirit of Christianity and of our ancient religion is essentially the same", and Standing Bear's truth-telling story (passage 10) illustrates "doing the truth" concretely. Passage 1's dawn prayer echoes Psalm 5:3 ("in the morning will I direct my prayer unto thee").
- **Ubuntu: _umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu_.** _Mitákuye oyás'iŋ_ and Ubuntu are both relational ontologies. The tension is that Ubuntu is primarily inter-human, while Lakota kinship explicitly includes non-human relatives.
- **Daoism: _Daodejing_ ch. 56** ("Those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know"). Eastman's "Silence is the cornerstone of character" and silent worship of the Great Mystery make a natural pairing.
- **Haudenosaunee Seventh Generation.** This is a sister Indigenous ethic of obligation across time. The Lakota counterpart is the concern for "family and descendants" in passage 8 and the thiyóšpaye.
- **Transcendentalism: Emerson, _Nature_ (1836).** Eastman's solitary communion in nature was written for an audience shaped by Emerson and Thoreau. The tension is that Emerson's nature is a symbol of spirit, while for the Lakota the beings of nature are relatives.
- **Islam / Abrahamic almsgiving (zakat, sadaqa).** The giveaway in passages 6 and 11 parallels Qur'an 2:261-262 on giving without reproach. "What you give away, you keep" echoes Matthew 6:19-20 (treasure in heaven).

## Beware

- **The "Chief Seattle speech"** ("The earth does not belong to man; man belongs to the earth... all things are connected") is not Lakota, and in its popular form not Seattle's either. It was largely written by screenwriter Ted Perry for a 1972 film, loosely based on an 1887 reconstruction by Henry A. Smith.
- **"Two Wolves" ("a Cherokee/Lakota grandfather tells his grandson...")** is a 20th-century evangelical sermon illustration, not a Lakota or Cherokee teaching.
- **"Great Spirit, grant that I may not criticize my neighbor until I have walked a mile in his moccasins"** is often labelled a "Sioux" or "Cheyenne prayer". The "walk a mile in his moccasins" line goes back to Mary T. Lathrap's 1895 poem "Judge Softly".
- **"Oh, Great Spirit, whose voice I hear in the winds..."** is attributed to "Chief Yellow Lark, Lakota, 1887". There is no reliable primary source, so treat it as unverified.
- **"We do not inherit the earth from our ancestors; we borrow it from our children"** is labelled a "Native American proverb" but has no traceable Indigenous source. It appears in 1970s environmentalist writing and is variously attributed to Wendell Berry and David Brower. (Note that the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's own site uses similar "borrowing" language. See haudenosaunee.md.)
- **"Native American Ten Commandments" / "Lakota Instructions for Living"** are modern New Age compilations with no tribal source.
- **Black Elk Speaks quotations** ("a people's dream died there"; "the power of the world always works in circles") are real lines of the 1932 book, but are often Neihardt's composition (per DeMallie). The book is also in copyright.
- **Eastman is Dakota, not Lakota.** Label his passages accordingly.
