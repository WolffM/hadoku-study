# Classical Greek ethics: Socrates (via Plato) and Aristotle

## Overview

Classical Greek ethics asks one question above all: _how should a human being live?_ (πῶς βιωτέον, _pōs biōteon_). The answer is framed around **eudaimonia** (εὐδαιμονία, "flourishing" or "happiness", literally "having a good guardian spirit"), and the way to it runs through **aretē** (ἀρετή, "virtue" or "excellence"): the qualities that make a person good at being human. The classical list of cardinal virtues comes from Plato: wisdom (σοφία / φρόνησις, _sophia_ / _phronēsis_), courage (ἀνδρεία, _andreia_), moderation (σωφροσύνη, _sōphrosynē_), and justice (δικαιοσύνη, _dikaiosynē_). The central concern is the condition of the **psychē** (ψυχή, "soul"). Socrates insists that care of the soul matters more than wealth, reputation, or bodily safety.

**Socrates** (469–399 BCE) wrote nothing. We know him mainly through the dialogues of his student **Plato** (c. 428–348 BCE), and to a lesser extent through Xenophon and Aristophanes. Scholars debate how much of the "Platonic Socrates" is historical. The early dialogues (_Apology_, _Crito_, _Euthyphro_) are usually thought closest to him. Certain Socratic claims are startling and remain influential: it is better to suffer injustice than to commit it; one must never return wrong for wrong; no one does wrong willingly; and "the unexamined life is not worth living." Socrates was executed by Athens in 399 BCE on charges of impiety and corrupting the young. His calm in the face of death (_Apology_, _Crito_, _Phaedo_) became a founding image of the philosophical life. Plato's _Republic_ adds the Allegory of the Cave, in which the Good is pictured as the sun, the source of light and truth. It also adds the Ring of Gyges thought experiment: would you still be just if you could never be seen?

**Aristotle** (384–322 BCE) was Plato's student and tutor to Alexander. In the _Nicomachean Ethics_ he grounds ethics in human nature and practice rather than in a transcendent Form. The human good is "activity of soul in accordance with virtue" over a complete life. Virtue of character (ἠθικὴ ἀρετή, _ēthikē aretē_) is a settled disposition (ἕξις, _hexis_) acquired by habituation (ἔθος, _ethos_). It aims at a **mean** (μεσότης, _mesotēs_) between excess and deficiency, and practical wisdom (φρόνησις) finds that mean. Aristotle gives two full books to **philia** (φιλία, friendship) and calls it indispensable to the good life. This "virtue ethics" shaped Hellenistic philosophy, Islamic philosophy (al-Fārābī, Ibn Rushd), Jewish thought (Maimonides) and Christian thought (Aquinas), and was revived in the 20th century (Anscombe, MacIntyre, Foot).

## Candidate passages

Text was copied from the fetched files. Only the original line breaks were joined. Curly apostrophes and quotation marks may have been typed as straight ones.

### 1. Plato, _Apology_ 29d–e

> Men of Athens, I honour and love you; but I shall obey God rather than you, and while I have life and strength I shall never cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy, exhorting any one whom I meet and saying to him after my manner: You, my friend,—a citizen of the great and mighty and wise city of Athens,—are you not ashamed of heaping up the greatest amount of money and honour and reputation, and caring so little about wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul, which you never regard or heed at all?

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1656/pg1656.txt
- **Why:** This is the Socratic mission in one sentence: care for the soul and for truth over wealth and reputation. "Obey God rather than you" closely echoes Acts 5:29.

### 2. Plato, _Apology_ 38a

> if I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1656/pg1656.txt
- **Why:** This is the real context of the most famous line in Greek ethics. It is self-examination as a daily practice, not a slogan.

### 3. Plato, _Apology_ 41c–d

> Wherefore, O judges, be of good cheer about death, and know of a certainty, that no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1656/pg1656.txt
- **Why:** Short and self-contained. It states Socrates' serene stance toward death on the day of his condemnation.

### 4. Plato, _Crito_ 49c

> Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1871), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1657/pg1657.txt
- **Why:** This is the earliest clear Western rejection of retaliation, four centuries before Matthew 5:39 and Romans 12:17. It is a powerful bridge passage.

### 5. Plato, _Gorgias_ 469b–c

> SOCRATES: That may very well be, inasmuch as doing injustice is the greatest of evils.
>
> POLUS: But is it the greatest? Is not suffering injustice a greater evil?
>
> SOCRATES: Certainly not.
>
> POLUS: Then would you rather suffer than do injustice?
>
> SOCRATES: I should not like either, but if I must choose between them, I would rather suffer than do.

- **Translation:** Jowett, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1672/pg1672.txt
- **Why:** This is the core Socratic paradox in a quick exchange. If space is tight, the last line works on its own.

### 6. Plato, _Republic_ II, 360c–d (Glaucon's Ring of Gyges)

> If you could imagine any one obtaining this power of becoming invisible, and never doing any wrong or touching what was another's, he would be thought by the lookers-on to be a most wretched idiot, although they would praise him to one another's faces, and keep up appearances with one another from a fear that they too might suffer injustice.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1497/pg1497.txt
- **Why:** This is the classic "would you be good if no one could see?" challenge. It pairs directly with the theme of John 3:19–21, where deeds are done in the dark or the light. Note that Glaucon is voicing a view he wants Socrates to refute. Caption it as a challenge, not as Plato's doctrine.

### 7. Plato, _Republic_ VII, 517b–c (the Cave interpreted)

> my opinion is that in the world of knowledge the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort; and, when seen, is also inferred to be the universal author of all things beautiful and right, parent of light and of the lord of light in this visible world, and the immediate source of reason and truth in the intellectual; and that this is the power upon which he who would act rationally either in public or private life must have his eye fixed.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1497/pg1497.txt
- **Why:** This is Greek philosophy's central light metaphor: the Good is the source of light and truth. It is the strongest bridge to John 3:19–21 and to the Qur'an's "Light Verse" (24:35).

### 8. Plato, _Republic_ X, 621c–d (closing words of the _Republic_)

> Wherefore my counsel is, that we hold fast ever to the heavenly way and follow after justice and virtue always, considering that the soul is immortal and able to endure every sort of good and every sort of evil. Thus shall we live dear to one another and to the gods, both while remaining here and when, like conquerors in the games who go round to gather gifts, we receive our reward.

- **Translation:** Jowett (1892), PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1497/pg1497.txt
- **Why:** This is a benediction that ends the whole _Republic_. It links justice, the soul, and death.

### 9. Plato, _Phaedo_ 118a (the last sentence)

> Such was the end, Echecrates, of our friend; concerning whom I may truly say, that of all the men of his time whom I have known, he was the wisest and justest and best.

- **Translation:** Jowett, PD
- **Source URL:** https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1658/pg1658.txt
- **Why:** This is the classic epitaph of Socrates. It is moving on a page about death, though it reads best with a one-line note on its context.

### 10. Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ I.7, 1098a16–20

> human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete. But we must add 'in a complete life.' For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy.

- **Translation:** W. D. Ross (1908), PD
- **Source URL:** http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html
- **Why:** This is Aristotle's definition of happiness together with the famous swallow image. It is short and quotable.

### 11. Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ II.1, 1103a32–b2

> For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.

- **Translation:** W. D. Ross (1908), PD
- **Source URL:** http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html
- **Why:** This is virtue as habit. It is the genuine source behind the fake "We are what we repeatedly do" quotation (see Beware).

### 12. Aristotle, _Nicomachean Ethics_ II.9, 1109a24–30

> any one can get angry- that is easy- or give or spend money; but to do this to the right person, to the right extent, at the right time, with the right motive, and in the right way, that is not for every one, nor is it easy; wherefore goodness is both rare and laudable and noble.

- **Translation:** W. D. Ross (1908), PD (the hyphen-dashes are as printed in the MIT text)
- **Source URL:** http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html
- **Why:** This is the doctrine of the mean made concrete and humane. It is often paraphrased, and this is the verifiable original.

**Also verified (short alternates):**

- _NE_ VIII.1, 1155a5 (Ross): "For without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods". Source: http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html
- _Republic_ VII, 517a (Jowett), on the prisoners who would kill their liberator: "and if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death." Source: Gutenberg #1497 (URL above). This is a striking parallel to John 3:19–20, where people hate the light.

## Beware (common misattributions)

- **"We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit."** This is Will Durant's paraphrase of _NE_ II in _The Story of Philosophy_ (1926). It is not Aristotle. Use _NE_ II.1 (#11) instead.
- **"It is the mark of an educated mind to be able to entertain a thought without accepting it."** This is not found in Aristotle. It is a loose modern paraphrase at best.
- **"Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a hard battle."** This is often attributed to Plato or Philo. It actually comes from 19th/20th-century sources, commonly credited to Ian Maclaren (Rev. John Watson).
- **"The only true wisdom is in knowing you know nothing."** This is a compressed paraphrase of _Apology_ 21d, not a verbatim line. The Jowett wording there is different.
- **"Only the dead have seen the end of war."** This is attributed to Plato but comes from George Santayana (1922).
- **"Wise men speak because they have something to say; fools because they have to say something."** This is not found in Plato.

## Bridges

1. **Light and the Good. Plato, _Republic_ VII 514–517 ↔ Christianity, John 3:19–21.** Both picture moral truth as light that people resist. In the Cave, the prisoners would kill whoever tries to "lead him up to the light" (517a). In John, "men loved the darkness rather than the light" because their deeds were evil. Plato's darkness is ignorance, while John's darkness is a will that hides. This tension is worth a caption.
2. **The invisible doer. Ring of Gyges (_Republic_ II 359–360) ↔ Stoicism, Seneca _Ep._ 43.4–5 ("If your deeds are honourable, let everybody know them"); Epicureanism, _Principal Doctrines_ 35; Christianity, Matthew 6:1–4.** Glaucon asks whether anyone is just unseen. Seneca answers with a conscience that welcomes the crowd. Epicurus answers with the impossibility of being _sure_ you are undetected. Jesus inverts the question: do good _in secret_, so the doing is not for show. These are four distinct answers to one question.
3. **Non-retaliation. _Crito_ 49c ↔ Christianity, Matthew 5:38–44 / Romans 12:17; Stoicism, Musonius Rufus _Discourse_ 10 and Marcus Aurelius VI.6; Buddhism, _Dhammapada_ 5 (hatred is not ended by hatred, only by non-hatred; paraphrase, verify wording in the Buddhist dossier).** Socrates' "we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil" is perhaps the earliest Western statement of this rule.
4. **Facing death. _Apology_ 40c–41d ↔ Epicureanism, _Letter to Menoeceus_ 124–125 ("death is nothing to us"); Norse, _Hávamál_ 76–77 (only the dead man's reputation lives on).** Socrates is agnostic between dreamless sleep and a journey, and is cheerful either way. Epicurus is certain it is annihilation, and is cheerful anyway. The Norse answer is that only fame (_orðstírr_) survives.
5. **The mean and the middle way. _NE_ II.6–9 ↔ Confucianism, _Doctrine of the Mean_ (_Zhongyong_); Buddhism, the Middle Way of the _Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta_.** These are similar in shape but differ in content. Aristotle's mean is a calibrated response to circumstances, not a halfway point. The Buddha's middle way lies between sensual indulgence and self-mortification.
6. **Humility of knowledge. _Apology_ 21d (Socratic ignorance) ↔ Hebrew Bible, Proverbs 3:7 (against being wise in one's own eyes; check WEB wording in the hebrew-bible dossier); Daoism, _Daodejing_ 71.** Recognizing your own ignorance is the start of wisdom.
